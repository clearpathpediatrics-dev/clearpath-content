How to Publish Research That AI Models Cite

October 5, 2026 · 7 min read

answer engine optimizationoriginal researchAI citationscontent strategydata-driven content

When an AI assistant answers a question, it reaches for sources that provide something specific: a number, a benchmark, a measured result. If your page is the one place a particular figure lives, you become the thing it quotes. That is the whole mechanism behind getting cited. Opinion pieces and rewrites of common knowledge get absorbed into the model's general soup. A page that says "the median repair cost across 480 jobs we logged was $312" gets attributed, because the model has nowhere else to pull that from.

Most businesses never try this because they assume original research means a university budget and a statistician. It doesn't. It means counting things you already touch, writing down what you find, and formatting it so a machine can lift it cleanly. Here's how to do that without a research department.

Why original data gets cited when opinion doesn't

Language models are pattern machines trained on enormous amounts of text. For any common claim, thousands of pages say roughly the same thing, so the model has no reason to attribute it to you. Your 900-word post on "signs your furnace is failing" competes with fifty thousand near-identical posts. The model learned the pattern; it doesn't need your page.

Original data breaks that symmetry. If you publish "in 1,200 furnace calls last winter, 38% of no-heat complaints traced to a failed igniter, not the blower," there is exactly one source for that sentence: you. When a user asks an assistant about common furnace failures, a model that has ingested your page has a reason to surface and name it. Specificity creates scarcity, and scarcity creates attribution.

This also holds up better over time. Opinions get copied and diluted. A proprietary dataset stays yours, and the more people cite it, the stronger the signal that your page is the origin.

What counts as research you can actually run

You do not need a survey panel. You need a question with a number at the end of it, and access to data that answers it. Most businesses are sitting on this without realizing it.

  • Operational logs. Service tickets, invoices, quotes, intake forms, support tickets. A plumbing company has pricing and job-type data across hundreds of calls. A law firm has case-type and timeline data.
  • Anonymized aggregates. You are reporting patterns, not individuals. "Average time from signed engagement to filing: 41 days" exposes no client.
  • Small purpose-built studies. Price-check 50 competitors in your city. Test 20 products and record failure rates. Time how long 100 tasks take.
  • Repeated benchmarks. Run the same measurement every quarter so you can publish "up from X last year," which is itself citable.

The bar is lower than people think. A dataset of 100 to 500 observations, honestly counted and clearly described, is enough to produce a figure no one else has.

A worked example

Suppose a commercial HVAC contractor in Phoenix wants a citable study. They pull their service tickets from the last 12 months — roughly 640 rooftop-unit calls. They categorize each by failure type and record the repair cost. In an afternoon in a spreadsheet, they produce:

  • Capacitor failures accounted for 29% of summer emergency calls.
  • Median emergency repair cost in July: $460, versus $280 in March.
  • Units over 12 years old generated 3.1x the call volume of units under 5 years.

Now they have three sentences no competitor and no general model can source elsewhere. Each becomes a potential quote in an AI answer to questions like "how much does commercial AC repair cost in summer" or "when should I replace a rooftop HVAC unit."

How do I format a research page so AI models can cite it?

Put the headline number in plain text near the top, state your sample size and method in one short section, and present each finding as a self-contained sentence with the number inside it. Models extract passages, not vibes, so every key figure needs to stand on its own without surrounding context.

Concretely, structure the page like this:

  1. Lead with the finding. First paragraph states the single most surprising number and what it's based on. "Across 640 rooftop-unit service calls we logged in 2024, capacitor failures caused 29% of summer emergencies."
  2. Add a methodology box. A short section: what you counted, how many, over what period, what you excluded. This is what makes it trustworthy to a human and parseable to a machine.
  3. Use a data table. Tables are easy for models to read and lift. One row per finding, with the metric and the value.
  4. Write each finding as a complete sentence. Avoid "as shown above" or "this number." Repeat the subject every time so the sentence survives being extracted alone.
  5. Date it and name yourself. "According to [Company]'s 2024 service-call analysis" gives the model the attribution string to reuse.

Numbers buried in a chart image are invisible to most text-based extraction. Always put the figure in writing too.

How long does it take to get cited after publishing?

There is no guaranteed timeline, and anyone who gives you one is guessing. Citation depends on the model ingesting your page, the page being indexed and crawlable, and the data being distinct enough to surface. In practice, pages that get picked up by human writers and linked to tend to appear in AI answers sooner, because models weight sources that others reference. That means distribution matters as much as the research itself.

To improve the odds, do three things after publishing. Get the study indexed quickly by submitting it and linking to it from pages that already get crawled often. Pitch the finding to journalists and niche newsletters who cover your industry, because an earned link to a data point is strong signal. And reference your own study in later articles, so the number recirculates across your site with consistent wording.

A publishing checklist

  • Pick one question with a numeric answer your data can support.
  • Pull and categorize at least 100 observations.
  • Write a methodology section: sample size, timeframe, what was excluded.
  • Lead the page with the single most interesting figure.
  • Put every key number in a plain-text sentence and in a table.
  • Date the study and include a reusable attribution phrase.
  • Link to it from three existing pages on your site.
  • Pitch the headline finding to two or three relevant publications.
  • Re-cite the number in future articles using the same wording.

Keeping it honest

The fastest way to lose a citation is to be caught inflating it. Don't round 100 tickets up to "thousands." Don't claim a national pattern from one city's data — say "across our Phoenix service area" and let the specificity be the strength. Report the sample size every time. A small, honest number beats a large, vague one, both for human trust and because models increasingly weight pages that show their work.

Refresh the study on a schedule. A number tied to "2024" ages; running the same count annually keeps the page current and gives you a year-over-year comparison, which is itself a new citable finding. The practical takeaway: pick one question this week, pull a few hundred rows of data you already have, and publish a single honest number nobody else can report. One well-formatted finding does more to get your business named in AI answers than a dozen general articles ever will.
