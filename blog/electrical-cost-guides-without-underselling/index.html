How to Write Electrical Cost Guides That Don't Undersell You

November 8, 2026 · 7 min read

electrical content marketingcost guide pageselectrician SEOpricing transparencyservice business content

A cost guide is one of the most searched and most valuable pages an electrician can own. People type "how much to rewire a house" or "cost to install an EV charger" because they are close to spending money and want to know what they are walking into. That intent is exactly why a bad cost guide does real damage. Publish a single low number with no context and you anchor every reader to that figure, then spend sales calls explaining why your quote is three times higher.

The goal is not to hide your prices. It is to show ranges that are accurate, explain what moves the number, and make the expensive version sound like the obviously correct choice for people who care about their property. Done right, the page qualifies leads before they call. Done wrong, it turns your inbox into a stream of "the guy down the road said $400" conversations.

Why a single number is the worst thing you can publish

Suppose a homeowner searches "cost to replace an electrical panel" and your page says $1,500. They read that number, screenshot it, and treat it as a ceiling. When your quote comes in at $2,800 because the service entrance needs upgrading and the meter base is corroded, you are now the expensive outlier arguing against your own website.

The fix is to never present a price without the conditions attached to it. A real panel replacement varies by amperage, panel location, whether the service drop needs upgrading, permit and inspection fees, and whether the existing wiring meets current code. A number without those variables is not information, it is a trap you set for yourself.

Ranges work better than single figures because they are honest and because they signal expertise. A reader who sees "$1,800 to $4,500 depending on amperage and whether your service entrance needs replacing" understands immediately that this is skilled work with real variables, not a flat commodity.

Build the page around what actually moves the price

Every electrical job has three or four factors that account for most of the cost variation. Name them. This is where you separate yourself from the handyman and the unlicensed competitor who quoted low because they skipped half the work.

For most electrical cost guides the price drivers fall into predictable categories:

  • Scope and capacity — amperage, number of circuits, square footage, load calculations
  • Access and conditions — crawl space versus finished walls, old knob-and-tube, aluminium wiring, asbestos-wrapped cable
  • Code and permits — permit fees, inspection requirements, upgrades triggered by bringing old work to current code
  • Materials and spec — standard breakers versus arc-fault/GFCI, panel brand, copper pricing
  • Labour — licensed electrician hours, whether a helper is needed, travel

When you explain these in plain language, the reader understands why two quotes for the "same" job can differ by thousands. You are also quietly pre-selling the value of a licensed, permitted job over a cash-under-the-table shortcut.

A worked example: EV charger installation

Here is how to lay out a single service so it informs without underselling. Suppose you install Level 2 EV chargers.

ScenarioTypical rangeWhat drives it
Charger near the panel, spare capacity$600 - $1,200Short wiring run, existing breaker space, surface mount
Charger across the garage or outside$1,200 - $2,500Longer conduit run, wall penetration, weatherproofing
Panel at capacity, needs a sub-panel or upgrade$2,500 - $5,000+Service upgrade, load calculation, permit and inspection

Underneath the table, explain the single most common surprise: many older homes do not have the spare panel capacity for a 40 or 50 amp circuit, which is why a $700 quote and a $3,500 quote can both be correct for the same charger. A reader who absorbs that will not be shocked by your estimate. They will expect it.

How do you show prices without scaring people off?

Show ranges tied to specific scenarios, lead with the value the price buys rather than the number alone, and name the cheap-option risks explicitly so readers self-select. People are not scared off by high numbers, they are scared off by numbers they cannot make sense of. A $4,000 figure with a clear explanation of what it includes feels safer than a $1,500 figure that might balloon.

Frame the spend against consequences the reader already worries about. For a panel replacement, that is fire risk from a failing bus bar, insurance companies refusing to cover certain panel brands, and the cost of doing it twice when a cheap job fails inspection. You are not fear-mongering. You are stating the real reasons the work costs what it costs.

It also helps to describe who each price tier is for. The low end suits a straightforward job in a newer home. The high end suits an older property that needs the service brought up to standard. When a reader places themselves in a tier, they arrive at your call already halfway sold on the right scope.

Should an electrician put prices on their website at all?

Yes, publish ranges with context, because the searches that include "cost" or "price" are among the highest-intent queries in the trade and ignoring them hands those leads to competitors and aggregator sites. The worry that competitors will undercut you is mostly unfounded, because an underpriced competitor was going to quote low whether or not your number was public. What you lose by staying silent is the chance to frame the conversation before anyone else does.

The one thing not to publish is a firm, bindable quote. Make clear the figures are typical ranges for planning purposes and that a real quote requires seeing the property. That protects you legally and sets the expectation that a site visit is part of the process, which it should be for anything beyond a simple fixture swap.

A checklist for a cost guide that holds its value

Before you publish, run the page against this list:

  1. Does every price appear as a range, never a lone figure?
  2. Have you named the three to five factors that move the price?
  3. Is there at least one table or scenario breakdown so readers can locate themselves?
  4. Did you explain why the cheapest option is often the wrong one, in concrete terms?
  5. Have you stated clearly that figures are estimates and a site visit produces the real quote?
  6. Does the page mention permits, inspection, and licensing where relevant?
  7. Is there a single clear next step for a reader who is ready — a call, a form, a booking link?
  8. Would the page still be accurate in twelve months, or did you tie it to a volatile input like copper spot price without a date?

That last point matters for maintenance. Prices drift. Add a "last reviewed" date and revisit cost guides once or twice a year. A guide quoting figures from three years ago undermines trust faster than no guide at all.

Interlink the guide so it works harder

A cost guide should not sit alone. Link it to the service page for that job, to any relevant permit or code explainer you have written, and to a comparison page if one fits. A reader who lands on "cost to rewire a house" often moves next to "signs your house needs rewiring" or "how long does a rewire take". Capturing that journey keeps the visitor on your site instead of returning to search results and finding a competitor.

The takeaway: a cost guide earns its keep when it makes the reader understand the work, not just the price. Lead with ranges, name the variables, explain why cheap is expensive, and end with a clear next step. You will field fewer price-shopping calls and more conversations with people who already understand why your number is what it is.
