How to Build a Seasonal Content Calendar

October 15, 2026 · 7 min read

seasonal content strategyannual content calendarcontent planningorganic visibilityeditorial calendar

The most common seasonal content mistake isn't writing the wrong topics. It's writing the right topics at the wrong time. A roofing company publishes its storm-damage guide in April, right when hail season starts, and wonders why it got no traffic from it. The page needed to be live and indexed in January to have any chance of ranking by the time people started searching.

Seasonality should shape your annual content plan in two ways: what you write and when you write it. Most planning gets the second part wrong. This piece walks through how to map your demand curve, work backward from your peaks, and keep a steady publishing cadence that doesn't leave you scrambling.

Why seasonal timing is a lead-time problem, not a calendar problem

New content does not rank the day it publishes. A fresh article on a competitive query usually spends weeks to a few months getting indexed, accumulating links and internal signals, and climbing from page three to somewhere useful. The exact timeline varies a lot by domain strength and competition, but the direction is reliable: ranking lags publishing.

That lag is the whole problem with seasonal content. Your busy season is a fixed deadline. If demand for "AC not cooling" spikes in June and July, a page published in June is competing from a standing start while it needs weeks of runway. The page that was published in March had time to settle before the traffic arrived.

So the planning question is never "what should we publish in July?" It's "what needs to be ranking in July, and when does it have to go live to get there?"

Step one: map your actual demand curve

Before you plan a single article, you need to know when demand actually peaks for your business. Don't guess from gut feel. Pull the real shape of the year.

  • Search trend data. Google Trends shows the seasonal shape of your core terms over a 12-month view. Look at "furnace repair," "tax preparation," "wedding venue near me" and you'll see the curve immediately.
  • Your own analytics. Twelve months of traffic and conversion data by month tells you when your site already gets busy, which is often tighter than the broad search trend.
  • Your sales records. When do quotes, bookings, and signed deals actually happen? There's often a gap between when people search and when they buy.

Write down the three or four distinct seasons your business has. Most have more than one. An HVAC company has a summer cooling peak, a fall heating pre-season, and a winter emergency-repair spike. A law firm doing estate planning might peak around year-end and again in tax season.

Step two: work backward from each peak

Once you know your peaks, assign each seasonal cluster a publish-by date that sits comfortably ahead of the demand it serves. A practical default is to have seasonal pages live and indexed 8 to 12 weeks before the demand curve starts climbing. For highly competitive terms, push that earlier.

Here's a worked example for a hypothetical HVAC company in Phoenix:

Summer cooling / AC repairEarly MayLate FebruaryMid-January
Fall maintenance tune-upsSeptemberEarly JulyEarly June
Winter heating repairNovemberEarly SeptemberEarly August

Notice how the drafting work stacks up months before anything feels urgent. In January, this company is writing about air conditioning while it's cold outside. That feels backward, which is exactly why most businesses don't do it, and exactly why the ones who do get the early-ranking advantage.

Step three: fill the gaps with evergreen work

If you only wrote seasonal content, your calendar would be lumpy. Every business has quiet stretches between peaks. Those weeks are for evergreen content: the explainer pages, comparison articles, and cost guides that pull steady traffic year-round and support your seasonal pages through internal links.

A good annual plan alternates. Use the pre-peak months to build and refine seasonal pages, and use the off-months to publish evergreen pieces that stay useful in any season. This keeps a consistent publishing cadence, which matters more for building domain momentum than any single burst of activity.

A simple two-track rhythm

  1. Seasonal track: scheduled to publish 8 to 12 weeks before each demand peak. Fixed deadlines, planned at the start of the year.
  2. Evergreen track: fills the weeks between seasonal deadlines. Flexible timing, drawn from your backlog of core question topics.

With both tracks running, you publish at a steady pace all year instead of panic-producing six articles in the two weeks before your season.

How far in advance should I publish seasonal content?

Publish seasonal content 8 to 12 weeks before demand starts climbing, and earlier for competitive terms. That window gives the page time to get indexed, gather internal link signals, and climb the rankings before the people searching for it arrive. If your term is low-competition and your site is already strong, you can tighten that to around six weeks. If the term is fought-over and your domain is young, three to four months isn't too early. The cost of publishing early is almost nothing; the page just sits there until traffic shows up. The cost of publishing late is the whole season.

Should I update last year's seasonal page or write a new one?

Update last year's page in almost every case, because a page that already has age, links, and ranking history will outperform a brand-new URL on the same topic. Every year you spin up a fresh "2025 AC maintenance guide" at a new URL, you throw away the authority the previous version earned and start from zero. Instead, keep the same URL, refresh the content, update any dates and pricing, add anything new, and republish it ahead of the season. If you must signal a new year in the title, change the on-page content and let the URL stay stable. The only time a new page makes sense is when the topic genuinely splits into two distinct searches that deserve separate pages.

A refresh checklist for returning seasonal pages

  • Update any year references in the title and body.
  • Check prices, regulations, and product details that may have changed.
  • Add any new questions customers started asking last season.
  • Re-check the target query in search; if the top results shifted intent, adjust.
  • Refresh internal links to and from newer pages you've since published.
  • Republish 8 to 12 weeks before the season, not during it.

Building the annual view

Put it all together on one page you can see at a glance. List each month down the side. For each month, mark which seasonal pages have publish-by deadlines that month, and which evergreen slots are open. The goal is to see your whole year before it starts, so no season sneaks up on you.

The hardest part of this isn't the strategy. It's the discipline to write about summer in winter, month after month, when nothing feels urgent. That's where a lot of in-house plans fall apart; the seasonal content keeps slipping because the deadline feels distant until suddenly it's too late. A structured program with a fixed cadence, whether you run it internally or through a service like ClearPath Content, exists mostly to keep that drift from happening.

The practical takeaway: map your demand peaks first, then set publish-by dates 8 to 12 weeks ahead of each one, and build backward from there. Fill the gaps with evergreen content so your cadence stays steady. Do that once at the start of the year, and you stop reacting to your calendar and start publishing on time, every season.
