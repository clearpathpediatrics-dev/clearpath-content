What to Publish in Your First 90 Days on a New Domain

October 4, 2026 · 7 min read

new domain seocontent strategyorganic visibilitycontent planningsite launch

A brand-new domain starts with nothing. No link history, no crawl pattern, no track record Google can use to decide whether your pages deserve to rank. Most people treat this as a reason to publish as much as possible, as fast as possible. That is usually a mistake. The first ninety days are about building a small, coherent body of work that signals what your site is for, then earning the early signals that let the rest of your content rank later.

What follows is a sequence, not a content calendar template. The dates are illustrative. The order and the reasoning behind each phase are the part worth copying.

Why a new domain behaves differently

A new domain has no demonstrated relevance for anything. When you publish a page, the search engine has no surrounding context to judge it against. An established site that has published 80 articles about commercial roofing gets the benefit of that history when it publishes article 81. Your new site does not.

This shows up as a lag. Pages get indexed, then sit at position 40 or 60 or nowhere for weeks before moving. People call this a sandbox. Whether or not that is a real, named mechanism, the pattern is consistent enough to plan around: new domains take time to earn trust, and you cannot shortcut it by publishing volume alone. Volume without coherence just gives the engine more low-signal pages to ignore.

So the goal of the first ninety days is narrow: establish topical identity, get a handful of pages indexed cleanly, and start accumulating the signals (links, engagement, internal structure) that make later content rank faster.

How many articles should you publish in the first 90 days?

For most small and mid-sized businesses, somewhere between 12 and 25 well-built pages in the first ninety days is a sensible target, not 100 thin ones. The exact number matters less than the shape of the set. You want a cluster that clearly covers one or two topics in depth rather than a scattered collection that touches twenty unrelated subjects. A plumbing company that publishes 15 pages all circling water heaters, drain problems, and emergency service reads as a water-and-drain authority. The same 15 pages split across plumbing, HVAC, electrical, remodeling, and general home tips reads as nothing in particular.

If you can only produce six genuinely good pages in ninety days, publish six. A tight, useful six beats a padded thirty every time on a new domain, because the engine is deciding what your site is about before it decides what it should rank for.

The three-phase sequence

Phase 1 (days 1-30): foundation and one tight cluster

Start with the pages that define the business and one topic cluster you can realistically own. Before any blog content, make sure the core site is in place: a clear homepage, service pages, an about page with real people on it, and contact details that match everywhere. These are not optional. A search engine assessing a new domain weighs whether this looks like a real business, and sparse or missing core pages undercut everything else you publish.

Then pick one cluster. Not three. One. Choose the topic where you have genuine expertise and where the search demand is specific enough that you are not competing head-on with national publishers on day one.

A worked example for a hypothetical commercial HVAC company in Columbus:

  • Pillar page: "Commercial HVAC maintenance contracts: what they cover and what they cost"
  • Supporting page: "How often should a rooftop unit be serviced?"
  • Supporting page: "Signs your commercial AC is about to fail"
  • Supporting page: "Preventive vs reactive HVAC maintenance for facilities"
  • Supporting page: "What a commercial HVAC inspection actually checks"

Five pages, all internally linked, all reinforcing one theme. The pillar links to each supporting page; each supporting page links back to the pillar and to one or two siblings where it genuinely helps the reader.

Phase 2 (days 31-60): depth plus first external signals

Extend the same cluster and start earning your first links. Add four to eight more pages that go deeper: cost breakdowns, comparison pages, decision guides, the specific questions buyers ask right before they call. This is also when you pursue the handful of links that move a new domain most: your local business listings, any trade associations you belong to, a supplier or partner page that will link to you, a chamber of commerce listing. These are not glamorous, but on a domain with zero history they do real work.

Resist the urge to open a second unrelated cluster yet. You want the first one to show movement before you split attention.

Phase 3 (days 61-90): second cluster and internal reinforcement

By now your earliest pages have been live long enough to show whether they are gaining traction in Search Console. Use that. If certain topics or query types are getting impressions, lean into them. Add a second cluster if the first is established, and spend real effort on internal linking across both so the site reads as a connected whole rather than a pile of posts.

What to measure in the first 90 days

Do not measure rankings or traffic as your primary signal this early, because both will be noisy and mostly flat. Measure the leading indicators instead:

  1. Indexation: Are your pages actually in the index? Check coverage in Search Console. Pages that are not indexed cannot rank, and new domains sometimes crawl slowly.
  2. Impressions: Growing impressions mean the engine is starting to show your pages for queries, even if you are not clicking-distance yet. This is the earliest real signal of traction.
  3. Query spread: Are you showing up for queries related to your cluster, or random unrelated terms? A tight query profile means your topical identity is landing.
  4. Average position trend: Direction over six weeks matters more than any single number. Position 55 moving to 35 is progress even though you are earning no clicks.

If impressions are climbing and the queries match your cluster by day 90, the foundation is working, even if traffic is still small. If you are indexed but getting no impressions at all, your topic may be too competitive for a new domain, and it is worth repointing at more specific queries.

Common mistakes that waste the first 90 days

The most expensive error is spreading thin across many topics. The second is publishing a burst of content in week one and then going quiet, which signals a dead or abandoned site. A steady cadence of one to three pages a week beats 20 pages in a single weekend followed by silence. The third is ignoring the core pages in favor of blog posts; a new domain with a weak homepage and no real service pages has no foundation for the articles to lean on.

The practical takeaway: treat your first ninety days as an identity-building exercise, not a volume contest. Pick one cluster, build it deep, link it tightly, earn a few real links, and watch impressions rather than rankings. The sites that win early are the ones that look like they know exactly what they are about, because that is the first thing a search engine has to figure out before it can rank you for anything.
