How to Earn Local Press Links That Help Rankings

November 10, 2026 · 7 min read

local SEOlocal press coveragebacklinksdigital PRlocal search visibility

Most local businesses never earn a single press link, and the ones that do usually get it by accident. That's a missed opportunity, because a link from a city newspaper, a regional business journal, or a local TV station's website carries more ranking weight than almost any directory you can buy your way into. These sites have age, authority, and local relevance baked in. One link from the right outlet can do more for your local visibility than fifty citations.

This piece covers what actually earns coverage, why those links help rankings, and a repeatable process you can run yourself without a PR retainer or a fabricated "study."

Why local press links move rankings at all

Search engines weigh links by the authority and relevance of the page pointing at you. A regional news site checks both boxes. It's an established domain that's been publishing for years, and it's geographically tied to the exact area you serve. When a Denver news site links to your Denver HVAC company, you're getting a relevance signal that a national directory can't match.

There are three separate benefits stacked in one link:

  • Authority transfer. The outlet's domain strength flows through the link to your page.
  • Local relevance. The outlet's own geographic signals reinforce that you belong in local results for that area.
  • Brand mentions. Even when a journalist names your business without linking, that mention is a signal engines and AI answer tools pick up on.

The catch: these links are hard to fake and hard to buy, which is exactly why they carry weight. You have to earn them by being genuinely useful to a reporter on a deadline.

What local journalists actually want

Reporters are not looking for your business. They're looking for a story, a source, or a local angle on something their editor already assigned. Your job is to be the easiest, most relevant person to quote or feature. That means framing yourself around what they cover, not what you sell.

Three things journalists reliably respond to:

  1. A local expert on a timely topic. A roofer who can explain what the latest hailstorm means for homeowners. A tax attorney who can translate a new state law into plain English. A dentist who can comment on a fluoride policy change.
  2. Real local data they can't get elsewhere. If you run a property management company and you can say "average rent on two-bedroom units in our portfolio rose 8% this year," that's a quotable, local, specific fact. Use only numbers you genuinely have.
  3. A human-interest hook tied to the community. A contractor who rebuilt a damaged community center at cost. A software firm that hired its tenth local apprentice. Not everything, but the genuinely newsworthy things.

Notice none of these is "we're having a sale" or "we won an industry award." Those get ignored.

How do I get my local business into the news without a PR firm?

Get your business into local news by making yourself a ready, relevant source for stories reporters are already writing, then pitching a specific person a specific angle at the right moment. You don't need a firm. You need a short list of local outlets, the names of the reporters who cover your beat, and a two-paragraph pitch that leads with the value to their readers rather than to you.

Here's the sequence that works for a single owner-operator with limited time:

  1. Build a source list. Find the 8 to 12 outlets that cover your area: the daily paper, weekly community papers, the regional business journal, local TV station sites, and any neighborhood blogs or newsletters with real readership.
  2. Identify the right reporter. Look at recent bylines. Find the person who covers housing, business, local government, or human interest, depending on your angle. Pitching the wrong person wastes both of your time.
  3. Watch for the moment. Register for a service that connects reporters with sources, and set alerts for weather events, new legislation, or seasonal stories tied to your field. A plumber's best week for coverage is the first hard freeze.
  4. Send a short, specific pitch. Two paragraphs. First: the angle and why their readers care right now. Second: who you are and one sentence proving you're qualified to comment. Offer to be available fast.
  5. Respond within the hour. Reporters work on deadlines measured in hours. The source who replies first and clearly usually gets quoted.

In early October, the owner emails the home and lifestyle reporter at the regional daily. Subject line: "Local plumber — how homeowners can avoid burst pipes before the first freeze." The pitch reads:

"With the first freeze usually hitting Spokane in mid-November, we get a wave of burst-pipe calls every year that are mostly preventable. I'd be happy to give your readers a quick checklist of what to do before temperatures drop, plus the two mistakes we see most often. I've been doing residential plumbing in the area for 14 years and can talk this week or next."

Attached: a five-item checklist the reporter can lift directly into the article. That last part matters. When you hand a reporter usable material, you make their job easier and raise the odds they credit and link you. If the article runs with "according to [Owner] of [Company]," you've earned a mention. If it links your site, you've earned the link. Either way you're now the plumber Spokane readers associate with freeze prep.

Run this four or five times a year around predictable local events and you build a genuine press footprint.

Do press mentions without a link still help SEO?

Yes, unlinked press mentions still help, though less directly than a link. Search engines and AI answer tools read your business name alongside your location and topic, and repeated associations across trusted local sites reinforce who you are and what you do. These mentions also feed the entity picture that local algorithms and AI assistants use to decide whether you're a credible answer to a local query. When possible, politely ask the reporter to link your name, but take the mention even if they decline. You can often follow up months later and get the link added, especially on evergreen articles.

Turning one piece of coverage into more

A single article is a starting point, not the finish. Once you've earned coverage, extend it:

  • Link to it from your own site on an "In the News" or "Press" page, which keeps the relationship visible and gives the outlet a reason to cover you again.
  • Reference the facts in your own content. If you were quoted on freeze prep, publish a fuller guide on your site and mention the coverage. This connects the authoritative mention to your deeper content.
  • Send the reporter a short thank-you and offer to be a standing source. Most local reporters keep a mental list of reliable people. Get on it.
  • Repurpose the angle for the next season with fresh data or a new hook.

This is slow, relationship-based work, which is why it holds up. Links you earn by being genuinely useful don't evaporate in the next algorithm update the way bought links do.

The same expertise that fills your site with useful articles makes you a quotable source, and the coverage you earn gives those articles authoritative links pointing back. The practical takeaway

You don't earn local press by broadcasting press releases. You earn it by being the most useful, most available local expert on a story a reporter is already writing. Build a list of 8 to 12 local outlets, find the reporter who covers your beat, watch for the predictable moments your expertise matters, and send a two-paragraph pitch that leads with reader value. Do that a handful of times a year, hand over material reporters can actually use, and the links will follow. They'll be few, but each one is worth more than most of what you could buy.
