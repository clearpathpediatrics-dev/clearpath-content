How to Write a Sentence AI Will Quote Back

August 9, 2026 · 7 min read

answer engine optimizationChatGPT SEOPerplexityAI searchcontent structure

When ChatGPT or Perplexity answers a question, it doesn't paste your paragraph. It lifts one or two sentences, sometimes a bullet, and stitches them into its own answer with a citation link back to you. If your page is written in long, hedged, context-dependent sentences, there's nothing clean to lift. The model summarizes you instead of quoting you, and the citation goes to a competitor who wrote a tighter line.

This is a mechanical problem, not a creativity problem. You can write sentences that are easy to extract, and doing so raises the odds your page becomes the cited source. Below is how the extraction actually works and the specific sentence patterns that survive it.

Why AI engines quote sentences, not pages

An answer engine builds a response by pulling candidate passages from several pages, ranking them for relevance to the exact question, and assembling the ones that stand on their own. The key phrase is stand on their own. A sentence that depends on the three paragraphs above it to make sense is a bad candidate, because the model can't drop it into an answer without importing all that context.

So the unit of competition is not the article. It's the self-contained sentence. Think of every page as a collection of potential pull-quotes. The more sentences you have that answer a specific question completely in one line, the more surface area you give the model to cite you.

What makes a sentence extractable

A quotable sentence has four properties:

  • It names its own subject. "It typically costs $150 to $300" is unquotable because "it" is unresolved. "A residential drain cleaning typically costs $150 to $300" can be lifted anywhere.
  • It contains the answer, not a promise of the answer. "There are several factors that affect pricing" is filler. "Pricing depends on three things: pipe length, access difficulty, and clog severity" is an answer.
  • It's short enough to reuse. Roughly 15 to 30 words. Long enough to be complete, short enough that a model won't need to trim it.
  • It carries a concrete detail. A number, a range, a named step, a specific condition. Concrete lines get quoted; vague ones get paraphrased away.

The question-then-answer pattern

The most reliable structure is a heading phrased as the exact question a person would type or speak, followed immediately by a sentence that answers it in full. Not a warm-up. The answer, first sentence, no throat-clearing.

Here's the difference on a hypothetical HVAC page.

Weak: "How long does a furnace last? This is a common question homeowners ask, and the answer can vary depending on a number of considerations. Let's take a closer look at what affects furnace longevity..."

Extractable: "How long does a furnace last? A gas furnace lasts 15 to 20 years with annual maintenance, and 10 to 12 years without it."

The second version puts a complete, cited-ready sentence directly under the question heading. A model scanning for "how long does a furnace last" finds the question in your heading and a clean answer one line down. That pairing is close to ideal for extraction.

The pattern in three steps

  1. Write the H2 as the literal question, in the words a customer uses. "How much does a will cost in Texas" beats "Understanding Estate Planning Fees."
  2. Make the first sentence beneath it a complete answer with a concrete detail. Subject named, number or specific included, under 30 words.
  3. Use the following sentences for the nuance, exceptions, and "it depends" material. That's where hedging belongs — after the clean answer, not instead of it.

How should I structure a page so ChatGPT quotes it?

Structure the page as a stack of self-contained question-and-answer blocks, each with a plain-language question heading and a direct answer in the first sentence below it. Lead every section with the answer, then expand. Keep answer sentences between 15 and 30 words, name the subject inside each sentence so it reads correctly out of context, and include one concrete detail — a price range, a timeframe, a step count — in the sentences you most want cited. Group related questions under one topic so the page covers a full cluster rather than a single query, because answer engines favor pages that resolve the whole question-space around a topic.

Does formatting like tables and lists help AI cite content?

Yes, tables and lists help because they package facts in a structure that's already extractable, and answer engines lift them directly. A three-column comparison table gets pulled into responses almost verbatim, because each row is a self-contained fact with its own labels. The same is true of numbered steps and short bulleted definitions. The caveat: the surrounding sentences still need to be clean, because the model often quotes a summarizing sentence alongside the table. A list of vague phrases won't rescue vague writing. Use lists for genuinely list-shaped information — steps, options, criteria — and keep each item to a complete thought.

A worked example: rewriting a section for extraction

Suppose a Tucson plumbing company has this paragraph on its water heater page:

Before: "When it comes to deciding whether to repair or replace your water heater, there are many things to consider. Age is important, and so is the cost of the repair versus a new unit. If you're unsure, it's always best to consult a professional who can assess your situation and recommend the right path forward."

Nothing there is quotable. No subject stays fixed, no number appears, and the "answer" is "call a professional." A model reading this has nothing to lift, so it will summarize a competitor instead.

After:

"Should you repair or replace a water heater? Replace a water heater when it's over 10 years old or the repair costs more than half the price of a new unit. A tank water heater lasts 8 to 12 years, so a major repair on a 10-year-old tank rarely pays off. Repair instead of replacing when the unit is under 6 years old and the problem is a single part, like a heating element or thermostat."

Every sentence names its subject, carries a number, and answers cleanly on its own. Any one of those three sentences could appear in a ChatGPT answer with a citation to the page. That's three quote candidates in one short block, versus zero in the original.

A pre-publish checklist

Run each section against this before it goes live:

  • Does the heading match a real question in customer language?
  • Does the first sentence below it answer that question completely?
  • Can each answer sentence be read alone without the words "it," "this," or "that" pointing at something offscreen?
  • Is there at least one number, range, timeframe, or named step in the sentences you most want cited?
  • Are your answer sentences under 30 words?
  • Is list-shaped information actually in a list or table?

If you're running an editorial program at any volume, this is the kind of structural discipline worth building into the brief itself rather than fixing after the fact — it's one of the standards we bake into pages at ClearPath Content, because retrofitting extractability across a whole site is slower than writing for it from the start.

The takeaway

Stop writing to fill space and start writing sentences that can be lifted. Put the question in the heading, the answer in the first line, the nuance after, and a concrete detail in every sentence you'd be proud to see quoted. Go back through your top five pages this week and rewrite the opening sentence of each section using that pattern. You'll notice immediately how much of your existing writing was warming up instead of answering — and warming up is exactly what the machines skip past.
