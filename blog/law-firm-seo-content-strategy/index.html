SEO Content Strategy for Law Firms That Gets Cases

August 26, 2026 · 8 min read

law firm SEOlegal content marketingpractice area pagesattorney content strategylaw firm blog

Most law firm websites have a blog full of posts like "Understanding Comparative Negligence" that get zero traffic and zero calls. The writing is fine. The problem is that it answers a question nobody types into a search bar when they need a lawyer. A person who just got rear-ended does not search "comparative negligence doctrine." They search "who pays if I got hit but was speeding."

A working SEO content strategy for a law firm starts from how people actually describe their legal problems, then builds pages around those descriptions in a structure search engines can navigate. Below is the approach that produces consultations instead of vanity pageviews.

Build your practice-area pages before you write a single blog post

Your money pages are your practice-area pages, not your blog. A personal injury firm needs a strong page for "car accident lawyer [city]," "truck accident lawyer [city]," "slip and fall lawyer [city]," and so on. These are the pages that capture people ready to hire. The blog exists to support them.

A common mistake is one thin "Personal Injury" page trying to rank for everything. Split it. Each distinct case type someone would search for as its own phrase deserves its own page. A firm that handles car accidents, motorcycle accidents, and wrongful death should have three separate pages, each 800 to 1,500 words, each answering the specific concerns that case type raises.

What belongs on a practice-area page

  • The specific problem in the client's words, in the first paragraph
  • How the process works from their side, step by step
  • What it costs to hire you and how fees work (contingency, flat, hourly)
  • What results look like without promising outcomes
  • Answers to the three or four objections that stop people from calling
  • A clear next step: call, form, or booked consult

Map the question-space around each practice area

Once your practice-area pages exist, you build blog content around the questions people ask before, during, and after a legal problem. This is where you catch people earlier in their thinking and earn trust before they're ready to hire.

Suppose a family law firm in Columbus handles divorce. The question-space around "divorce" is enormous and specific:

  • How long does a divorce take in Ohio?
  • Do I have to go to court to get divorced?
  • How is a house divided in an Ohio divorce?
  • Can I get divorced without my spouse agreeing?
  • What does an uncontested divorce cost?
  • Who gets the dog in a divorce?

Each of these is a real search with a person behind it. Each becomes an article that answers the question directly, then links to the divorce practice-area page. The person reading "how long does a divorce take in Ohio" is not ready to sign today, but they will be, and you want to be the firm that already answered their question honestly.

How to find the actual questions

  1. Ask your intake staff what callers ask in the first two minutes. Those are your highest-intent questions.
  2. Type your practice area into a search bar and read the autocomplete suggestions and the "people also ask" boxes.
  3. Read the questions on legal forums and Q&A sites for your practice area and jurisdiction.
  4. Check the emails clients send after they've hired you. Repeated questions are content.

How many articles should a law firm publish per month?

Publish two to four well-researched articles a month, consistently, rather than ten thin ones in a burst. Consistency matters more than volume for a law firm because you're building topical depth in a narrow set of practice areas, and search engines reward a site that clearly demonstrates expertise across a subject over time. A firm that publishes four solid, 1,000-word pieces a month, each answering a genuine client question and interlinked to the relevant practice-area page, will out-perform a firm that dumps twenty generic posts and stops. The goal is to eventually cover the full question-space of each practice area you want cases in, then keep those pages current as laws change.

Write for the reader, then satisfy the compliance concern

Lawyers are trained to hedge, qualify, and cite. That instinct produces content that is accurate and unreadable. The fix is not to be inaccurate. It's to lead with the plain answer, then add the necessary qualification.

Compare these two openings to "can I get divorced without my spouse agreeing?":

Weak: "The dissolution of a marital union in circumstances where one party does not consent involves several procedural considerations that vary by jurisdiction and fact pattern."

Strong: "Yes. In Ohio you can get divorced even if your spouse refuses to agree or won't participate. It takes longer and the process is different from an uncontested divorce, but a spouse cannot block a divorce by refusing to cooperate. Here's how it works."

The strong version answers the question in the first sentence, which is what the reader came for and what AI answer engines pull into featured snippets. Every hedge you need can come after the answer, not before it.

Interlink so every article feeds a practice-area page

An article that ranks but has no path to a consult is wasted. Every blog post should link to at least one practice-area page using descriptive anchor text, and each practice-area page should link back out to the supporting articles that cluster around it. This does two things: it guides the reader toward hiring you, and it signals to search engines that these pages belong together as a body of expertise.

A simple structure for a personal injury firm:

Hub pageSupporting articles that link to it
Car Accident LawyerWhat to do after a car accident; How long do I have to file; Do I need a lawyer for a minor accident; Who pays my medical bills
Truck Accident LawyerWhy truck cases are different; Who is liable in a truck accident; What a trucking company's insurer does after a crash

A four-week starting sequence

If you're starting from nothing, here's a concrete order of operations for a single practice area:

  1. Week 1: Write or rewrite the main practice-area page. Make it specific, honest about cost, and clear about the next step.
  2. Week 2: Publish the two highest-intent question articles your intake staff hears most. Link both to the practice-area page.
  3. Week 3: Publish two more articles covering process and cost questions. Add internal links from the practice-area page back to all four articles.
  4. Week 4: Review what's indexed, fix any page missing a clear call to action, and plan the next month's four questions.

Repeat for your next practice area. Depth in one area beats a thin spread across five.

Don't ignore the trust signals search engines and clients look for

Legal content sits in a category where search engines apply extra scrutiny, because bad legal information can hurt people. That means the surrounding signals matter as much as the writing: attorney bios with real credentials and bar admissions, author attribution on articles, accurate contact information, and content that stays current with the law. An article about filing deadlines that cites a rule changed two years ago does more harm than good. Build a review step into your process so a licensed attorney at the firm signs off on anything covering law or procedure.

This is also where an outside content program earns its keep. A team like ClearPath Content can map the question-space and produce the drafts in your firm's voice, but the accuracy review has to stay with a lawyer who knows the current law in your jurisdiction. The division of labor works when the writing is outsourced and the legal sign-off is not.

The takeaway: stop writing law review articles and start answering the exact questions your future clients type when they're scared and looking for help. Strong practice-area pages, a growing library of plain-language answers around each one, honest first-sentence answers, and tight internal linking will do more for your caseload than any amount of jargon-heavy content. Pick one practice area, build the hub, answer the top questions, and repeat.
