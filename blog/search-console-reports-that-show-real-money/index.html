Which Search Console Reports Show You Real Money

August 14, 2026 · 8 min read

search consolecontent roiorganic marketingsearch analyticslead generation

Search Console is free, and most business owners look at it exactly twice: once when someone sets it up, and once when traffic drops and they panic. The problem is not the tool. The problem is that the default view answers a question nobody at a business actually asks: "how many total clicks did the whole site get?" That number tells you nothing about whether search is bringing you buyers.

The good news is that with three or four filters you can turn the same free reports into something that answers real questions: Which pages bring people who are ready to hire? Which queries do I show up for but lose the click on? Where am I one small fix away from a first-page position? Here is how to set it up so it earns its place in your week.

Start by throwing out the top-line number

The headline "total clicks" chart is the least useful thing on the screen. A dental practice can double its clicks by ranking for "do teeth whitening strips work" — a question asked by people who will never book a cleaning. Meanwhile the page that quietly brings in three new-patient calls a month might get 40 clicks total.

So the first move is to stop measuring volume and start measuring intent-weighted performance. That means separating your pages and queries into two buckets: money pages (services, locations, pricing, comparison, "near me") and top-of-funnel pages (definitions, how-tos, general questions). You judge each bucket by different standards. Money pages should convert clicks into contacts. Top-of-funnel pages exist to build authority and feed internal links — you do not expect them to book jobs directly.

The four filters that make Search Console useful

Open the Performance report and turn on all four metrics at the top: Clicks, Impressions, Average CTR, and Average Position. Then set the date range to the last three months and compare it to the previous three months. Now apply these filters one at a time.

1. Filter by your money-page URLs

Use the "Page" filter and paste in the URL path for a service or location page. Now you are looking at how one earning page performs, not the whole blog. If your "emergency-ac-repair" page gets 800 impressions but a 1.2% click-through rate, the page ranks but the title and description are not convincing anyone to click. That is a copywriting fix, not a ranking problem — and it is one you can make in ten minutes.

2. Filter queries by buyer language

Use the "Query" filter set to "contains" and try words like cost, price, near me, quote, vs, and your city name. These queries carry commercial intent. If you show impressions for "HVAC repair cost tucson" but sit in position 14, that is a query worth a dedicated page or a section on an existing one.

3. Filter for positions 8 to 20

There is no position filter, so sort the query table by Average Position and scan the rows between roughly 8 and 20. These are the queries where you are on page one or two but not yet visible enough to earn clicks. Moving from position 12 to position 6 usually produces a bigger click gain than moving from position 3 to position 1. This is your highest-return work list.

4. Filter by device and country

If you are a local business, filter to your country and check the mobile-versus-desktop split. Local service searches skew heavily mobile. If a key page ranks well on desktop but poorly on mobile, that often points to a page-speed or layout issue that is costing you the people most likely to call.

How do I know which pages actually bring in leads?

Connect Search Console to the page where inquiries happen, then match query data to that page's conversions. Search Console shows you clicks and impressions, but it does not know when someone filled out a form or called. To close that gap, you line up two things: the Search Console "Page" report and whatever records your contacts (a form tool, a call-tracking number, or your CRM's source field).

Here is a practical way to do it without fancy software. Once a month, list your top ten landing pages by clicks from the Search Console Page report. Next to each, write how many inquiries that page produced — from form notifications, call logs, or a simple "how did you find us" question you ask on the phone. Now you can see the truth: a page might rank fifth for clicks but first for booked jobs. That is the page you protect and expand. A page with heavy clicks and zero inquiries is either wrong-intent traffic or a page that fails to ask for the contact.

If you use Google Analytics, connect it to Search Console under Settings, and set up a conversion event for your contact form and phone-click. That gives you query-to-conversion data automatically, but the manual method above works fine for most small businesses and forces you to actually look.

A worked example: reading one page correctly

Suppose a law firm in Columbus has a page for "probate lawyer columbus." The default Search Console view says it got 210 clicks last quarter — fine, unremarkable. Now filter to that page and read the detail:

  • Impressions: 6,400
  • Clicks: 210
  • Average CTR: 3.3%
  • Average position: 7.5

Three findings jump out. First, position 7.5 means the page usually sits at the bottom of page one — real traffic is available if it moves up two or three spots. Second, a 3.3% CTR at that position is low; a page titled and described well at position 7 should pull closer to 5-6%. Third, when you cross-check the intake log, that page produced four consultations last quarter. Four consultations from a probate practice is not a rounding error — it is meaningful revenue.

The action list writes itself: rewrite the title and meta description to raise CTR (fast win), add a section answering the "how much does probate cost in Ohio" queries the report shows as impressions-only (captures adjacent intent), and add internal links from related blog posts to push the page's authority up a few positions. None of that required a new tool or a bigger budget.

How often should I check Search Console?

Check it once a month for decisions and once a quarter for strategy — daily checking tells you nothing but noise. Search data is volatile day to day, and staring at it invites overreaction to normal fluctuation. A monthly review, using the four filters above, is enough to catch the things that matter: a page that dropped in position, a new query gaining impressions, a money page with a weak click-through rate.

Set a recurring 30-minute block. Pull the three-month comparison, run your four filters, and write down no more than three actions. The discipline of limiting yourself to three keeps you fixing high-value problems instead of tinkering.

Put it into a simple monthly checklist

  1. Compare last 3 months to the previous 3, all four metrics on.
  2. Filter to each money page — flag any with CTR under 2% at position 10 or better.
  3. Filter queries containing cost, price, near me, and your city — note commercial queries where you rank 8-20.
  4. Sort queries by position; list the page-one-bottom opportunities.
  5. Match your top ten pages against your inquiry log.
  6. Write down three actions. Stop.

This is roughly the reporting discipline we build into client programs at ClearPath Content — connecting query data to what a page is actually meant to do, rather than reporting clicks for the sake of a chart.

The practical takeaway: Search Console already contains the answers to your real business questions, but only if you stop reading the top-line number and start filtering by intent and matching pages to inquiries. Thirty minutes a month, three actions, and you will spend your effort on the pages that bring buyers instead of the ones that bring browsers.
