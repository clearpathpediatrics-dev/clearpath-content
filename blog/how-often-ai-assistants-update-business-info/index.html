How Often AI Assistants Update Info About You

November 8, 2026 · 7 min read

answer engine optimizationai searchbusiness listingslocal seocontent freshness

A dentist changes her phone number, updates it everywhere she can think of, and three weeks later a patient tells her ChatGPT gave them the old number. This happens constantly, and the reason is that AI assistants don't hold a single, live picture of your business. They stitch together information from different sources, each with its own refresh schedule, and some of those sources update in minutes while others lag for months.

If you understand which source feeds which answer, you can stop guessing and start fixing the slow spots. Here's how the refresh cycle actually works and what you can do about it.

There is no single "AI memory" to update

When someone asks an assistant about your business, the answer can come from three very different places, and they move at different speeds.

  • Training data — the frozen snapshot the model was trained on. This is the slowest layer. A model released this year may have a knowledge cutoff from months earlier, and it won't change again until the next model version ships. If an assistant "knows" you from training data alone, it could be working from a picture that's a year old.
  • Live retrieval / web search — most major assistants now fetch live pages when a question looks current, local, or factual. ChatGPT search, Google AI Mode, Perplexity, and Copilot all do versions of this. This layer reflects whatever the search index has crawled recently, which can be hours to weeks old.
  • Structured data partners — business directories, maps platforms, and licensed data feeds. When an assistant shows your hours, phone, or address, it's often pulling from one of these, not from the model's own memory.

The practical takeaway: the fastest way to change what an assistant says about you is almost never "wait for the model to retrain." It's to change the sources the live layers read from.

How often does each source actually refresh?

The honest answer is that it varies by source, but the general pattern is consistent and worth internalizing. Here's how the layers typically compare.

SourceTypical refresh speedWhat it affects
Google Business ProfileMinutes to a few daysHours, phone, address, services in local answers
Your own websiteDepends on crawl frequency — days to weeksFacts the assistant pulls live from your pages
Third-party directoriesWeeks to monthsSecondary facts, reconciliation against other sources
Search index (live retrieval)Hours to weeks after a page changesAnything the assistant fetches in real time
Model training dataMonths, only on new model releasesBaseline "memory" when no live source is used

Two things fall out of this table. First, the data you control directly — your profile and your site — are the fastest to move, which is good news. Second, the slow layers tend to win when the fast layers are silent or contradictory. An assistant that can't find a confident current answer will fall back on stale training data or an old directory entry.

How long until AI assistants show my updated business hours?

Updated business hours usually appear in AI answers within a few days if you change them at the primary source, but a full refresh across every assistant can take two to six weeks. The speed depends entirely on where you make the change. Update your Google Business Profile and the local-answer layer often reflects it within days, because that platform pushes changes quickly. Update only your website and you're waiting on the next crawl plus the assistant's retrieval timing. Update nothing but a third-party listing and you may wait a month or more.

The lag gets worse when sources disagree. If your website says you close at 5, your profile says 6, and an old directory says 4, the assistant has to pick — and it may hedge, show the wrong one, or say it's uncertain. Consistency across sources isn't just tidy; it removes the ambiguity that makes assistants cling to outdated data.

A worked example: changing your phone number

Suppose an HVAC company in Tucson switches to a new tracking number and wants assistants to stop handing out the old one. Here's the sequence that moves fastest, in order of impact.

  1. Google Business Profile first. Change the primary phone number here before anything else. This is the single highest-leverage edit for local answers and usually propagates within days.
  2. Your website, everywhere. Update the header, footer, contact page, and any schema markup. Don't leave the old number sitting in a PDF or an old blog post — assistants that read live pages will find it and may surface it.
  3. Major directories. Update the big aggregators and any industry-specific listings. These reconcile slowly, but if you skip them they become the stale source that contradicts your fast ones.
  4. Kill the old number's footprint. Search your own business name plus the old number. Anywhere it still appears, fix it or request a correction. This is the step most businesses skip, and it's why old numbers linger.
  5. Wait and re-check. Give it two to three weeks, then ask the assistants directly: "What's the phone number for [business]?" If an assistant still shows the old one, look at which page it cites — that tells you exactly which source to fix.

That last step matters. When an assistant cites a source, it's showing you its homework. Follow the citation, fix that page, and you've addressed the actual cause instead of guessing.

Why fresh, consistent content speeds the whole thing up

The live retrieval layer is where you have the most control, and it rewards pages that are current, specific, and clearly about your business. An assistant deciding whether to trust your website over an old directory is making a confidence judgment. Pages that are detailed, recently updated, and internally consistent read as more authoritative than a thin contact page that hasn't changed in two years.

This is where regular publishing earns its keep beyond rankings. A business that publishes dated, specific content — answering real customer questions, naming its service areas, stating its hours and policies plainly — gives assistants a steady stream of fresh, crawlable signals. When your facts change, there's an active, frequently-crawled surface for the update to land on. A dormant site gives the crawler no reason to come back soon, so your corrections sit unseen.

Does it help to just ask the AI to update its information?

No — telling an assistant "please update my hours" in a chat does not change what it knows, because that correction isn't stored and doesn't feed back into any data source. The model has no memory of your request after the conversation ends, and it has no mechanism to edit the directories or index it reads from. The only reliable way to change the answer is to change the underlying source the assistant pulls from. Correcting the data at the source and giving the live layers something fresh to crawl is slower than a chat message, but it's the only thing that sticks.

A quick audit you can run today

Set aside twenty minutes and run through this. It surfaces most refresh problems before customers do.

  • Ask three different assistants for your hours, phone, and address. Note any that are wrong.
  • For each wrong answer, ask the assistant where it got that information.
  • Check that your Google Business Profile matches your website exactly.
  • Search your business name plus your old phone number or old address to find stale copies.
  • Confirm your contact details are in plain text on your site, not buried in an image.

If you'd rather have someone keep the content layer fed and consistent on a cadence, that's the kind of ongoing editorial work ClearPath Content handles — but the audit above is free, and running it yourself will tell you most of what you need to know.

The core idea is simple: AI assistants don't update on one clock. The sources you control move in days, the ones you don't move in months, and stale sources win when fresh ones go quiet. Keep your primary profile and your website accurate and active, chase down old copies of changed facts, and let the citations tell you where the next fix belongs.
