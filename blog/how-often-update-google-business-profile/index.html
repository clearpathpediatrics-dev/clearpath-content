How Often Should You Update Google Business Profile?

July 31, 2026 · 7 min read

google business profilelocal seogbp optimisationlocal search visibilitygoogle maps ranking

Most Google Business Profiles get filled out once, verified, and then left alone. The owner ticks it off the list and moves on. Six months later the profile still ranks about where it started, and the conclusion is that GBP "doesn't do much."

The problem usually isn't the setup. It's the neglect. Google's local algorithm reads activity and freshness signals, and a profile that hasn't changed since launch looks dormant next to a competitor who posts, adds photos, and answers questions every week. But most advice on this points you toward the wrong activity, telling you to post daily when posts barely move ranking, while ignoring the fields that do.

This piece sorts the maintenance that matters from the maintenance that doesn't, and gives you a cadence you can actually keep.

How often should you update your Google Business Profile?

Update the high-impact fields whenever the facts change, and run a light maintenance pass roughly every two weeks, with a deeper review once a quarter. There is no ranking benefit to daily fiddling, and Google does not reward busywork. What it responds to is a profile that stays accurate, keeps accumulating photos and reviews, and shows signs of an active business behind it.

Think of it in three tiers:

  • Event-driven: hours, address, phone, services, and categories. Update the moment they change. A wrong holiday hour or a stale phone number does real damage, both to ranking and to the customer standing outside your locked door.
  • Every one to two weeks: add a few photos, respond to new reviews, answer any questions in the Q&A section, and clear out spam. Fifteen minutes.
  • Quarterly: audit categories against competitors, rewrite the business description if your service mix has shifted, and check that your service-area or map pin still makes sense.

That's the whole rhythm. If you're doing more than this, you're probably chasing signals that don't exist.

Which fields actually affect local ranking

Google's local ranking rests on three broad factors: relevance, distance, and prominence. Distance you can't change. The other two you can influence, and only a handful of profile fields do the influencing.

The primary category is the single most important field

Your primary category tells Google what you fundamentally are, and it carries more weight than any keyword you stuff into your description. A dental practice set to "Dentist" competes for different searches than one set to "Cosmetic dentist" or "Emergency dental service." Pick the one that matches the money you actually want to make, not the broadest label.

Then add secondary categories for your real service lines. A general contractor might add "Deck builder," "Bathroom remodeler," and "Kitchen remodeler." Each secondary category makes you eligible to appear for a cluster of searches you'd otherwise miss. Don't pad the list with categories you don't serve, but don't leave money on the table by listing only one either.

Services and their descriptions feed relevance

Under each category you can list individual services and write a short description for each. These fields are underused and they help you rank for specific service queries. If you're an HVAC company, don't just have "HVAC." List "AC repair," "furnace installation," "heat pump replacement," "duct cleaning," each with a sentence or two in plain language describing what it involves.

Reviews carry prominence, and so does responding to them

Review count, review velocity, and the words inside reviews all feed ranking. A steady trickle of reviews signals an active business better than a burst of twenty followed by silence. Responding to reviews matters too, both because it adds keyword-relevant text to the profile and because it signals the business is being run. Respond to all of them, positive and negative, within a few days.

Photos signal an active, real business

Profiles with regularly added photos tend to hold up better than static ones. You don't need a photographer. Job-site photos from a phone, before-and-afters, the team, the storefront, finished work. Add a handful every couple of weeks. The freshness is the point as much as the content.

What doesn't move the needle as much as people think

Google Posts are the biggest offender here. They're worth doing, but mostly for the customer who's already looking at your profile and might click an offer, not for ranking. Posting three times a day will not out-rank a competitor with better categories and more reviews. Post when you have something genuinely worth saying, weekly is plenty.

The business description field is another one people obsess over. It's a 750-character box that appears well down the profile. Write it clearly, include your main services naturally, and move on. It's not a ranking lever you can crank.

Booking links, attributes, and product listings all have their place for conversion, but treating them as ranking factors is a misallocation of your hour. Fix the categories and reviews first.

A realistic monthly maintenance checklist

Here's the sequence a busy owner or office manager can run. Budget about an hour a month total, split across the two-week touches.

  1. Confirm the core facts. Hours correct, including upcoming holidays. Phone and address exact. Website link working. Two minutes.
  2. Respond to every new review. Thank the good ones by name and mention the service. Address the bad ones calmly. Ten minutes if you've kept up.
  3. Add three to five photos. Recent work, team, premises. Name the files descriptively before uploading where you can.
  4. Check the Q&A section. Answer real questions. Google lets anyone post answers, so seed the common ones yourself and delete spam.
  5. Scan for suggested edits. Google and the public can suggest changes to your profile. Check nobody has altered your hours or category. This happens more than owners realise.
  6. Once a quarter, audit categories. Look at the three top-ranking competitors for your main search. Note their primary and secondary categories using a browser tool or by inspecting their profile, and make sure you're not missing an obvious one.

How long before profile changes affect ranking?

Category and core-field changes can register within days to a couple of weeks, while the compounding effects of reviews and photos build over months. Local ranking is not instant, and it's not a switch. If you fix your primary category today, Google may re-evaluate your relevance for the associated searches fairly quickly. But the prominence signals, the steady reviews and consistent activity, accumulate slowly and are what separate you from a competitor over a quarter or two.

This is also why the cadence matters more than any single edit. A profile that's maintained every two weeks for a year beats one that gets a heroic overhaul and then goes quiet. Consistency is the mechanism.

A worked example

Suppose a two-location dental practice in Columbus has both profiles set to primary category "Dentist," a description written at launch, forty reviews on one location and eight on the other, and the last photo added eleven months ago.

The fix sequence: set the flagship location's primary category to match its highest-value service, add secondary categories for "Cosmetic dentist," "Teeth whitening service," and "Emergency dental service." List individual services under each with short descriptions. Start asking every patient at checkout for a review, aiming for a handful a week rather than a campaign. Add job and team photos every fortnight. Respond to the existing backlog of reviews.

None of that is exotic. It's the same six-step checklist run on a neglected profile. The point isn't a clever tactic, it's closing the gap between a profile that was set up once and one that's actually maintained.

The takeaway

Your Google Business Profile isn't a form you complete, it's an asset you maintain. Get the primary category right, list your real services, keep reviews and photos accumulating, and run a fifteen-minute pass every couple of weeks. Skip the daily posting anxiety. The businesses that win local search aren't doing anything secret. They're just the ones who never let the profile go quiet.
