Who Actually Writes Your Content Service's Articles?

November 25, 2026 · 7 min read

content marketingcontent agencybuying guidesghostwritingAI content

When a content service quotes you $299 a month for four articles, a reasonable question is: who sits down and writes those articles? The answer determines what you are actually buying. It also explains why two services at the same price can produce work that looks nothing alike.

Most buyers never ask. The sales conversation is about topics, cadence, and reporting dashboards. The production model stays hidden, and you only discover it after the first draft lands in your inbox reading like it was assembled by someone who has never seen your business. This piece walks through the models that exist, how to tell them apart, and the specific questions that cut through the pitch.

The four production models you are actually choosing between

Nearly every content service runs on one of these arrangements. The label on the website rarely tells you which.

1. In-house staff writers

A salaried team, usually 3 to 15 people, writing for a fixed roster of clients. Quality tends to be consistent because the same person handles your account month after month and learns your voice. It is also the most expensive model to run, which is why you mostly see it at agencies charging $2,000+ per month, not at subscription price points.

2. A managed freelance network

The service maintains a bench of contract writers and assigns your work to whoever is available and roughly matches your industry. An editor reviews drafts before they reach you. This is the most common model at the $200 to $800 range. Quality swings with who got assigned, and your voice resets whenever a writer rotates off.

3. AI-first with a human pass

A language model generates the draft, a human edits it for accuracy and tone, and the service bills it as written content. Done honestly, this is a legitimate workflow. Done lazily, the human pass is a 90-second skim and you receive text that reads clean but says nothing specific about your market. The price point looks like freelance; the margins are much higher.

4. Offshore content mills

Volume writing produced at low per-word rates, often by writers for whom English is a second language, with light or no editing. You can usually spot it: grammatically correct sentences that explain nothing a practitioner would recognize as true about the trade.

None of these is automatically wrong. A good managed freelance writer beats a bored in-house staffer. A careful AI-plus-editor workflow beats a rushed content mill. The problem is paying for one model and silently receiving another.

How do I find out who writes the content before I sign up?

Ask to name the specific person who will write your articles, and ask to see three samples that same person wrote for a client in a different industry. A service running real writers can answer in one email. A service that assigns work to a rotating pool or leans on AI will get vague: "our team," "our vetted writers," "a writer matched to your niche." That vagueness is the answer.

  2. Ask for writer-specific samples. Not the agency's best portfolio piece, but three articles the person assigned to you actually produced.
  3. Ask about the editing step. "Who reviews the draft before I see it, and what are they checking for?" A real editor has a checklist. A nonexistent one produces a shrug.
  4. Submit one trade-specific fact in your onboarding and see if it appears. Tell them something only an insider knows, then check whether the first draft uses it or ignores it.
  5. Run the first draft through a plain read-aloud test. If it sounds like a Wikipedia summary of your industry rather than something you would say to a customer, you have your answer about who, or what, wrote it.

What a real worked example looks like

Suppose a commercial electrician in Columbus signs two trials, one with each of two services, same brief: an article on "why a panel upgrade costs more in older homes."

Service A returns 1,100 words that correctly define a panel upgrade, list general cost factors, and close with a call to action. Accurate, readable, and generic. It never mentions knob-and-tube wiring, the permit inspection that older homes trip, or the fact that opening a wall in a 1920s build frequently reveals surprises that change the quote. That is a draft written by someone, or something, with no field knowledge and no questions asked.

Service B returns 1,000 words that lead with the three things an electrician actually finds in older Columbus homes that drive the price: outdated wiring that has to be addressed to pass inspection, undersized service entrances, and cramped meter locations. It reads like the electrician talking. Somewhere in the production chain, a person who understood the trade either wrote it or interviewed someone who did.

Same price, same word count, completely different thing purchased. The difference is not "AI versus human." It is whether the production model includes a step where someone with domain knowledge shapes the content. Service B could have used AI for the first draft and still won, because a knowledgeable editor rebuilt it. Service A could have used a human writer and still lost, because no one in the chain knew the trade.

Does it matter if AI writes the first draft?

It matters far less than whether a knowledgeable human controls the output. A draft generated by a language model and then rebuilt by an editor who knows your industry can be excellent. A draft typed by a human freelancer with no trade knowledge and no one checking their work can be useless. The deciding factor is not the tool that produced the first words; it is whether the final article contains specific, correct, non-obvious things about your business and market. Judge the output, not the origin.

That said, you deserve to know. If a service uses AI, ask them to say so plainly and describe their editing process. A straight answer here is itself a signal. Services that hide the AI step tend to hide the thin editing step too.

A quick checklist for the sales call

  • Can you name the person writing my articles? One name, not "our team."
  • Does that person stay on my account, or rotate?
  • Can I see three samples that specific person wrote?
  • Do you use AI in the draft stage? What does a human do after?
  • Who edits, and what is on their checklist?
  • If I give you an insider fact, will it show up in the draft?
  • What happens when my regular writer is unavailable?

Any service worth paying can answer all seven without stalling. The ones that deflect are telling you the production model is something they would rather you not examine.

The takeaway

Stop asking content services how many articles you get and start asking who produces them and who checks them. The number of words is commodity information. The production chain, who writes, who edits, whether anyone in the process knows your trade, is what separates content that sounds like you from content that sounds like every competitor who bought the same package. Ask the seven questions, demand writer-specific samples, and judge the first draft on whether it contains something only an insider would know. If it does not, it does not matter who, or what, wrote it.
