SEO Content for Mortgage Brokers: What to Write

October 25, 2026 · 8 min read

mortgage broker SEOmortgage content marketingSEO for lendersmortgage broker marketingfinancial services content

A mortgage borrower does an enormous amount of research before they ever call a broker. They want to know what credit score they need, whether they can use a bonus as income, how much a self-employed buyer can borrow, and whether it's worth refinancing to drop PMI. Each of those is a search. Each search is a chance to be the one who answered the question clearly.

Most broker websites skip all of this. They have a homepage, an "about" page, a rates widget, and a contact form. That's a brochure, not a content strategy. Below is how to build content that actually captures the research phase and turns it into applications.

Why mortgage content is different from most local service content

Two things make mortgage content harder than, say, content for a plumber or a dentist.

First, the information is regulated and consequential. Someone acting on wrong guidance about debt-to-income limits or loan programs can make a six-figure mistake. Google knows this. Mortgage sits squarely in the "Your Money or Your Life" category, which means the bar for accuracy, author expertise, and trust signals is higher than for most industries. Vague, AI-spun filler gets nowhere.

Second, the facts move. Rates change daily. Loan limits reset annually. Program rules shift. A guide written in confident present tense about "current rates" is wrong within a week. This forces a structural decision: separate the content that ages fast from the content that ages slowly, and build so the fast-aging stuff is quarantined.

Map the question-space before you write anything

Borrowers cluster into a handful of situations, and each situation has a predictable set of questions. Start by listing the borrower types you actually serve, then write out the questions each one asks in their own words.

First-time buyers

  • How much do I need for a down payment?
  • What credit score do I need to buy a house?
  • What's the difference between pre-qualification and pre-approval?
  • How much house can I afford on [X] salary?
  • What closing costs should I expect?

Self-employed and commission borrowers

  • Can I get a mortgage if I'm self-employed?
  • How do lenders calculate income from a 1099?
  • What is a bank statement loan and who qualifies?
  • How many years of tax returns do I need?

Refinance and equity

  • When is it worth refinancing?
  • How do I remove PMI from my loan?
  • What is a cash-out refinance and how does it work?
  • How long does a refinance take?

Local and program-specific

  • First-time buyer grants in [your state]
  • FHA loan limits in [your county]
  • Jumbo loan requirements in [your metro]

The local and program-specific questions are where a broker has an advantage over national comparison sites. A national site will never write a clear, current page on "down payment assistance programs in Mecklenburg County." You can.

Should mortgage brokers put current rates in their content?

No, not as the core of a written article, because rate numbers go stale immediately and a page built around them becomes a liability the moment the market moves. If you want to show rates, use a live widget or a feed that updates on its own, and keep it off your evergreen guides entirely. Your written content should explain the mechanics that don't change: how rate locks work, what drives your rate up or down, how points affect the number, why your quote differs from the advertised teaser rate. That material stays accurate for years and still answers the real question behind "what are rates today," which is usually "how do I get a good one and not get burned."

A simple rule: if a sentence contains a specific rate, loan limit, or dollar figure that changes on a schedule, either pull it into a clearly dated callout or replace it with the mechanism. "The 2024 conforming limit is $X" ages badly. "Conforming limits are set annually by the FHFA and rise in most years; here's how to check the current number for your county" does not.

How many articles does a mortgage broker actually need?

Enough to cover each borrower situation end to end, which in practice means 25 to 40 well-built pages for a single-market broker, not a few hundred. Depth beats volume here. One thorough, genuinely helpful guide on self-employed mortgages that walks through income calculation, documentation, and common denial reasons will outperform ten thin posts that each skim the surface. Mortgage is competitive, and the winning pages tend to be the ones that fully resolve the question so the reader doesn't need another tab open.

Build in clusters. A pillar page on "self-employed mortgages" links down to supporting pieces on bank statement loans, 1099 income, and tax return requirements, and each of those links back up. This interlinking tells search engines the pages belong together and helps readers move through the topic without hitting the back button.

A 10-article starter sequence

If you're starting from a brochure site, this order front-loads the pages that tend to convert and covers the widest borrower base first.

  1. How much house can I afford? — top-of-funnel, huge volume, leads naturally to a pre-approval CTA.
  2. Pre-qualification vs pre-approval — clears up the single most common point of confusion.
  3. What credit score you need to buy a home — high-intent, and lets you address repair timelines.
  4. Down payment assistance in [your state] — local, hard for national sites to match.
  5. Closing costs explained, line by line — reduces the sticker shock that kills deals.
  6. Can you get a mortgage while self-employed? — underserved, high-value borrower segment.
  7. When refinancing is worth it — captures existing homeowners, not just buyers.
  8. How to remove PMI — specific, searchable, and a natural refinance lead-in.
  9. FHA vs conventional loans — comparison intent, easy to make genuinely useful.
  10. What to expect from application to closing — sets expectations and positions you as the guide.

Publish on a steady cadence rather than dumping all ten at once. A new, substantive page every week or two signals an active site and gives you time to interlink each piece properly as the library grows.

Get the trust signals right

Because mortgage is a YMYL topic, the page around the content matters as much as the words. A few concrete things to put in place:

  • Named author with credentials. Put a real licensed loan officer's name on the byline, with their NMLS number and a short bio. Anonymous financial advice underperforms.
  • Last-updated dates. Show when a page was last reviewed. For anything touching numbers, review quarterly.
  • Clear, honest framing. If a program has downsides, say so. Pages that acknowledge tradeoffs read as more credible to both humans and the systems ranking them.
  • Compliance review. Build a quick check into your process so nothing goes live with language your regulator would flag. One reviewer, a short checklist, done before publish.

A worked example

Suppose a broker in Boise serves a lot of self-employed clients from the local trades and tech scene. The pillar is "Self-Employed Mortgages in Idaho." It explains, in plain terms, how a lender averages two years of net business income, why write-offs that lower your tax bill also lower your qualifying income, and what documentation to gather. Underneath it sit three supporting pages: one on bank statement loans, one on how 1099 income is calculated, and one on the top reasons self-employed applications get denied.

Every page carries the loan officer's byline and NMLS number, a last-reviewed date, and a single clear next step: book a 15-minute call. No rate numbers anywhere in the body. When conforming limits change in January, the only edits needed are to one dated callout, not to the whole library. That's the payoff of separating the durable from the perishable at the structure level. The takeaway

Write for the situation, not the keyword. Pick your three or four most valuable borrower types, answer every real question each one has, keep rate numbers out of your evergreen pages, and put a named, licensed author on everything. Do that across 25 to 40 solid pages and you'll be answering questions at the exact moment someone decides whether to call a broker — which is the only moment that matters.
