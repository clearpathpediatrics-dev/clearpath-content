What a Good Content Report Should Show You Monthly

November 13, 2026 · 7 min read

content reportingcontent marketing metricscontent providerSEO reportingbuying guide

If you are paying someone $199 to $499 a month to publish articles, the report they send is the only window you have into whether the money is working. Most reports fail this job. They lead with traffic charts that go up and to the right, a screenshot of a rankings tool, and a line that says "great progress this month." None of that tells you whether the program is moving you toward more customers.

A useful report answers three plain questions: what did you produce, what is it doing, and what are you doing next. Everything else is decoration. Here is how to read the report you get now and what to demand if it comes up short.

What should a monthly content report actually include?

A monthly content report should include four things: what was published, how each piece is performing against the query it was built for, the leading indicators that predict future results, and a specific plan for the next month. If your report only covers the first item and a total traffic number, you are getting an activity log, not a performance report.

Break those four into line items you can check:

  • Production ledger — every URL published that month, the target query or question for each, and word count or a link to the live page. You should be able to click and read what you paid for.
  • Per-article performance — impressions, clicks, average position, and the query each article is actually surfacing for. Not a site total. The per-page view is where you catch articles that rank for the wrong thing.
  • Leading indicators — new queries the site is appearing for, pages that moved from page three to page one, and internal links added. These predict next quarter before the traffic shows up.
  • The plan — what gets written next month and why, plus any pages flagged for updating or consolidation.

That is the whole job. If it fits on two pages, good. Length is not quality.

The metrics that matter versus the ones that pad the report

Reports get long because padding is easy and substance is hard. Learn to separate the two fast.

Metrics worth your attention

  • Impressions by query — shows what you are becoming visible for, even before clicks arrive. A rising impression count on relevant commercial queries is the earliest honest signal.
  • Clicks to money pages — articles exist to send readers somewhere that converts. Track whether the new content drives visits to service pages, contact forms, or booking flows.
  • Position movement on target queries — the specific queries each article was built for, tracked over time. Movement from position 18 to position 7 matters more than a page already sitting at 40 that nobody clicks.
  • Conversions or inquiries you can attribute — form fills, calls, demo requests. This is imperfect because attribution is messy, but any attempt is better than none.

Metrics that are mostly noise

  • Total sessions with no breakdown — a sitewide number that can rise because of a brand mention, a newsletter, or a seasonal fluke that has nothing to do with the content program.
  • Bounce rate as a standalone verdict — a reader who got their answer and left is not a failure.
  • Domain authority scores — a third-party vendor's invented number, not something any search engine uses.
  • Social shares — unless social is your channel, this is filler.
  • "Keywords ranking" as a raw count — ranking for 4,000 keywords means nothing if they are all position 60 on terms no customer searches.

A simple test: if a line in the report cannot change a decision you make, it does not belong in the report.

Red flags in a monthly content report

Some report habits signal that a provider is managing your perception rather than your results.

  • No per-article data. If everything is reported at the site level, individual failures stay hidden. This is the most common way weak work gets masked.
  • Screenshots instead of exportable data. A cropped image of a rankings tool is easy to cherry-pick. Ask for the underlying export or access to the console.
  • Year-over-year comparisons used to dodge recent months. "Traffic is up 40% versus last year" can be true while the last three months flatlined.
  • No mention of anything that did not work. Every real program has articles that miss. A report with no misses is either filtered or not being read by the person who wrote it.
  • The plan never changes based on the data. If the next-month section looks the same regardless of what the numbers said, nobody is actually using the report to steer.

How often should you get a content report, and who should read it?

You should get a written report monthly, and the person who owns revenue should read it, not just forward it. Monthly is the right cadence because organic content moves slowly — weekly reports mostly show noise, and quarterly reports let problems run too long before anyone catches them. A month is enough time for new articles to start registering impressions and for last month's flagged fixes to show movement.

The reader matters as much as the schedule. Content reports often go to a marketing coordinator who files them. The business owner or the person who answers "did we get more customers" should spend ten minutes on it. You do not need to understand every metric. You need to check that the production matches what you paid for, that the per-article numbers trend the right way over several months, and that the plan responds to the evidence. If a provider resists giving you direct access to Search Console or an analytics view, treat that as a reason to ask harder questions.

A good provider welcomes this. At ClearPath Content we would rather a client read the report critically than nod at a traffic chart, because the programs that last are the ones where the client can see the mechanics working.

The takeaway

Judge the report, not the design. Open yours and check for four things: a clickable list of what was published, per-article performance against named queries, leading indicators like new impressions and position movement, and a next-month plan that reacts to the numbers. If those four are present and honest, you can tell within ten minutes a month whether your content program is earning its fee. If they are missing, the polish is hiding something — and you are the one paying for it.
