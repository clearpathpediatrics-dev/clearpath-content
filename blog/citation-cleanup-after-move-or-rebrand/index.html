Fixing Your Business Citations After a Move

September 25, 2026 · 8 min read

citation cleanuplocal searchNAP consistencybusiness moverebrandGoogle Business Profile

A move or a rebrand breaks something most owners never think about until rankings slip: your citations. A citation is any place online that lists your business name, address, and phone number — directories, data aggregators, review sites, chamber pages, old profiles you forgot you created. When those details were correct, they reinforced each other. The day you change your address or name, half of them go stale and start contradicting the other half.

Search engines read that contradiction as uncertainty. If one source says you are on Main Street and another says Oak Avenue, and a third still uses your old company name, the engine has no clean signal to trust. That uncertainty shows up as lower local pack placement, fewer map impressions, and phone calls going to a disconnected number.

This is fixable, but the order matters and the busywork is real. Here is how to do it without missing the listings that actually move the needle.

Why stale citations hurt more than you expect

The damage is not just the one wrong listing. It is the fragmentation. Suppose a dental practice in Columbus rebrands from "Smith Family Dental" to "Bright Arch Dentistry" and moves two miles across town. If 40 directories still carry the old name and address, the practice now effectively has two identities online, each with partial data and split reviews.

Search engines try to consolidate signals into one business entity. Conflicting name, address, and phone (NAP) data makes that consolidation harder, so the engine either picks the wrong version or hedges by ranking you lower. Customers hit the friction too — they call a dead number, drive to an empty suite, or leave a review on a profile you no longer watch.

The phone number is usually the worst offender in a move because people keep the old one live "just in case" or, worse, let it get reassigned. A number that now rings a different business is an active liability, not a loose end.

Build your source-of-truth record first

Before you touch a single listing, write down the exact version of your details you want everywhere. This sounds trivial. It is the step people skip, and it is why cleanups drift.

Lock these down in one document:

  • Business name — the exact spelling, capitalization, and whether you include "LLC" or "Inc." Pick one form and never vary it.
  • Address — including suite format. Decide "Ste 200" versus "Suite 200" and use it consistently.
  • Phone — one primary local number. If you are porting the old number, confirm the port completed before you start updating listings.
  • Website URL — with or without "www," trailing slash conventions settled.
  • Categories and hours — so you correct them once while you are in each profile anyway.

Every edit you make from here forward matches this record character for character. Inconsistent suite abbreviations alone can keep two versions of your listing from merging.

Fix listings in priority order, not alphabetical order

Not all citations carry the same weight. Working through them randomly wastes hours on directories nobody reads. Go in this sequence.

1. Google Business Profile

This is the single most important listing. Update the address, name, phone, and hours directly. If you moved, do not create a new profile — edit the existing one so you keep your review history and profile age. Expect a reverification step (postcard, phone, or video) after an address change. Do this first because Google is also a source many other sites pull from.

2. The core data aggregators

A handful of data providers feed hundreds of smaller directories. In the US that means Data Axle, Localeze/Neustar, and Foursquare. If old data lives at the aggregator level, it will keep repopulating the small directories no matter how many times you fix them manually. Correct the source, then the downstream listings follow over weeks.

3. Bing Places and Apple Maps

Apple Maps matters more than most owners assume because it powers directions and search on iPhones and Siri. Bing feeds some AI answer tools. Both are quick to update.

4. High-traffic vertical and review sites

Yelp, industry directories, your chamber of commerce, and any site where you have real reviews. Reviews are tied to the profile, so an abandoned old profile is stranded social proof.

5. Everything else you can find

The long tail of smaller directories. Fix them, but do not lose a weekend over a site with no traffic.

How do I find all my old citations?

Start with a Google search of your old phone number in quotes and your old business name in quotes, because those exact strings surface listings that scrapers and directories have copied. Search "555-123-4567" and "Smith Family Dental" and page through the results — each hit is a citation to check. Then run the same searches with your old address. A paid citation-tracking tool speeds this up by scanning known directories at once, but the manual search catches the odd forum post, old press release, or supplier page a tool misses. Keep a spreadsheet with columns for the site, the URL, current data shown, correct data, and status. That spreadsheet is your project plan and your proof of completion.

Should I keep my old phone number after moving?

Port your old number to the new location if you possibly can, rather than switching to a fresh one, because every citation, business card, and inbound link tied to the old number keeps working during the transition. If you must change numbers, set up call forwarding from the old number for at least six to twelve months and update your highest-priority listings immediately so new callers reach the current line. The risk with abandoning a number is reassignment — carriers recycle disconnected numbers, and you do not want your listings pointing customers to a stranger's phone. Treat the number change as the trigger for the whole citation sweep, not a separate task.

A worked cleanup sequence

Here is a realistic order of operations for a rebrand-plus-move, using a hypothetical HVAC company:

  1. Week 1: Finalize the source-of-truth record. Confirm the phone port or forwarding is live.
  2. Week 1: Update Google Business Profile. Start reverification. Do not delete the old profile.
  3. Week 2: Submit corrections to the three main data aggregators.
  4. Week 2: Fix Bing Places and Apple Maps.
  5. Week 3: Run the exact-match searches on old name, old number, old address. Build the full citation spreadsheet.
  6. Weeks 3-5: Work the spreadsheet in traffic-priority order. Claim or update each listing to match the record exactly.
  7. Week 6: Update your own website — footer, contact page, schema markup, location pages — so your site broadcasts the same NAP.
  8. Weeks 8-12: Re-run the searches. Downstream directories that pull from aggregators should now show corrected data. Chase the stragglers.

The website step in week 6 is easy to forget and quietly important. Your own site is a citation, and its structured data is one of the clearest signals an engine reads. If your footer still says the old address, you are contradicting your own cleanup.

What to check when you think you are done

Search your old details one more time a month after finishing. Directories cache aggressively, and a listing you fixed can revert if an aggregator still held bad data when the site last synced. Duplicate profiles are the other common leftover — a move sometimes spawns a second auto-generated Google listing that needs merging or removal.

Cleaning up citations is slow, unglamorous work that pays off in a signal engines can finally trust. If you would rather not run the spreadsheet yourself, this is the kind of groundwork ClearPath Content handles as part of a local visibility program, though a determined owner can absolutely do it in-house.

The takeaway: a move or rebrand does not just change your sign — it fractures your online identity across dozens of listings. Lock down one exact version of your details, fix the high-weight sources first, correct the data aggregators so the long tail follows, and re-check a month later. Consistency is the whole game.
