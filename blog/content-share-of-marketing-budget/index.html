How Much of Your Marketing Budget Goes to Content?

October 22, 2026 · 7 min read

content budgetmarketing budget allocationcontent marketing costsmall business marketingbuying guide

Most budget questions about content start in the wrong place. Owners ask "how much should an article cost?" when the real question is "what share of what I already spend should go here, and what do I pull back to pay for it?" Content doesn't get a free dollar. It competes with your ads, your lead-gen tools, your trade memberships, and the time your team spends on marketing. So the useful framing isn't a price tag. It's a percentage, and then a swap.

This piece gives you a way to set that percentage, benchmarks by business type so you know if you're an outlier, and a worked example you can copy with your own numbers.

Start with your real marketing number, not your gut

Before you can split a budget, you need to know its size. Pull the last twelve months and add up everything that exists to generate demand: paid search and social, directory listings and lead-gen subscriptions (think Angi, Thumbtack, Avvo, Clutch), your website hosting and any retainer you pay a developer, email tools, CRM seats that exist mostly for marketing, event and sponsorship costs, print, and any agency or freelancer fees.

Two numbers matter after that. First, total marketing spend as a share of revenue. Service businesses commonly land somewhere between 5% and 12% of revenue, higher when they're growing fast or in a crowded market, lower when most work comes from referrals. Second, the split between rented attention (ads and lead-gen you pay for every click) and owned attention (your site, your content, your email list). Content lives on the owned side, and most businesses that have been burned by agencies are badly overweight on the rented side without realizing it.

What share of marketing spend should content be?

A reasonable starting range is 15% to 30% of your total marketing budget for content, with most established local service businesses sitting around 20%. If you're brand new and need leads this month, start lower — content is slow, and you can't pay payroll with a page that ranks in nine months. If your ad costs have climbed to the point where each lead is painful, and you plan to be in business for years, push toward the higher end because owned assets compound while ad spend resets to zero every month you stop paying.

Here's how the range tends to break down by situation:

SituationContent as % of marketing budgetWhy
New business, need leads now10-15%Ads buy time; content builds slowly in the background
Established, ad costs rising20-30%Reduce dependence on per-click costs
Strong brand, mostly referrals15-20%Capture the searches referrals already send you
Competitive market, long sales cycle25-35%Buyers research for weeks; content is where they land

These are starting points, not laws. The number you choose matters less than choosing it on purpose and reviewing it every quarter.

A worked example you can copy

Suppose a dental practice in Columbus does $1.4M a year and spends 8% of revenue on marketing. That's $112,000 annually, or about $9,300 a month. When they actually itemize it, here's what they find:

  • Google Ads: $4,500/mo
  • A lead-gen directory subscription: $1,200/mo
  • Website hosting and a developer retainer: $800/mo
  • Email and CRM tools: $600/mo
  • Print mailers, two drops a year: averaged to $900/mo
  • Sponsorships and local events: $500/mo
  • Content: $800/mo on occasional blog posts a freelancer writes when someone remembers to ask

Content is roughly 8.5% of their marketing budget, and it's ad hoc. If they decide content should be 20%, that's about $1,860 a month. The honest question is where the extra $1,060 comes from. It shouldn't be new money bolted on top; it should be a swap.

The best candidate is usually the weakest-performing rented channel. If the directory subscription produces leads they could capture organically — people searching "emergency dentist near me" or "invisalign cost Columbus" — then shifting part of that $1,200 toward content that targets those exact searches reduces a recurring rental cost and builds an asset they own. A sensible move: trim the directory by $600, trim print mailers by $450, and put both into a consistent content program. No increase to total spend, and the mix tilts toward owned attention.

The point of the exercise isn't the specific dentist. It's the sequence: itemize, find your current content share, pick a target share, then fund the increase by cutting your lowest-value rented channel rather than raising the total.

How do you decide what to cut to pay for content?

Cut from the channel with the highest cost per acquired customer and the least durability — usually a lead-gen directory or broad-match ads on terms you could rank for organically. Score each marketing line on three things: what it costs per customer actually won (not per lead, per customer), whether it keeps working if you stop paying, and whether it's defensible against competitors who can simply outbid you. Rented channels fail the second test by definition. Directory subscriptions often fail the third, because the directory sells the same lead to your competitors. Content is slow and uncertain in its own ways, but a page that ranks keeps producing after it's paid for, which is the quality you're buying.

A quick scoring pass:

  1. List every marketing line item and its monthly cost.
  2. For each, estimate customers won per month from it. Be honest; guess low where you can't track it.
  3. Divide cost by customers to get cost per customer.
  4. Mark each line "stops when I stop paying" or "keeps working."
  5. Fund your content target by trimming the worst line that also stops when you stop paying.

What content actually costs at each budget level

Knowing your share only helps if you know what it buys. Roughly, consistent long-form content — the kind that targets specific buyer questions and gets interlinked — runs $150 to $500 per article depending on research depth, length, and whether it's written in your voice or templated. A program that publishes four to eight pieces a month, with a plan behind which topics to cover and how they connect, tends to land in the $600 to $2,000 per month range for a local or regional business.

Translate that back to percentages. If your total marketing budget is $9,300 a month and you want content at 20%, your $1,860 buys a steady program with planning and interlinking, not just stray posts. If your budget is $3,000 a month, 20% is $600, which buys a lighter cadence — plan accordingly and expect slower compounding. Review the number, don't set and forget it

Revisit your content share every quarter against one question: is the owned side growing? You're watching for organic traffic to pages that match buyer intent, for those pages starting to produce calls or form fills, and for your cost per customer on rented channels. If organic is picking up real demand, you can usually trim ads further and move that money to content, compounding the shift. If six to nine months pass with no movement at all, the problem is rarely the budget percentage — it's topic selection or publishing consistency, and that's worth diagnosing before you change the dollar figure.

Takeaway: Pick a content share on purpose — 15% to 30% of marketing for most established businesses — fund the increase by cutting your weakest rented channel rather than adding new spend, and re-score the mix every quarter. The specific percentage matters less than treating content as a line you fund deliberately and an asset you own, instead of the thing that gets money only when it's left over.
