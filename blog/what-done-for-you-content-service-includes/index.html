What 'Done For You' Should Mean in Content

October 29, 2026 · 7 min read

done for you contentcontent service comparisoncontent subscriptionbuying guidecontent marketing

"Done for you" is one of the most overused phrases in content marketing, and one of the least defined. Two providers can both use it on their pricing page and mean completely different amounts of work. One hands you finished articles that go live on your site without you lifting a finger. The other emails you Google Docs and considers their part done.

The gap between those two is where most of the frustration lives. You think you bought a service that runs itself. Three weeks in, you discover you are the one formatting posts, adding images, writing title tags, and chasing your web developer to publish. That is not done for you. That is done halfway.

This piece breaks the phrase into its actual components so you can hold any provider to a real standard before you sign.

The eight things a complete service actually covers

Strip away the marketing language and a genuine end-to-end content service handles all of the following. If a provider does fewer than these, the remainder lands on your desk.

  1. Topic research. Someone figures out what your market is actually searching and asking, not just what sounds good in a meeting. This means mapping the question-space around your services, not picking topics off the top of someone's head.
  2. A planned schedule. A cadence you can see in advance, so you know what is coming and when, instead of pieces arriving at random.
  3. Writing in your voice. Drafts that sound like your business, informed by how you actually describe your work, not generic filler that could belong to any company in your trade.
  4. Editing and fact-checking. A second pass that catches errors, tightens the prose, and verifies claims. One person writing with no review is not a process.
  5. On-page optimisation. Title tags, meta descriptions, headings, and internal structure set up correctly, not left blank for you to fill in.
  6. Internal linking. New pieces connected to your service pages and to each other, so the content works as a system rather than a pile of orphaned posts.
  7. Publishing. The article going live on your site, formatted properly, with the person doing it actually logging into your CMS.
  8. Reporting. A plain summary of what was published and how it is performing, so you can judge whether the money is working.

You will notice that only three of these are "writing." The other five are the operational work that turns a document into a published, discoverable page. Providers who cut corners almost always cut them here, because this is the part the client does not see in the sample.

The four gaps that quietly become your job

When a service is incomplete, the missing work does not disappear. It transfers to you. These are the four most common handoffs that providers leave out while still calling the service "done for you."

Publishing access

If the provider will not touch your CMS, every article becomes a task for you or your developer. For a business publishing four posts a month, that is roughly an hour of copy-paste-format-publish work per piece, so four to five hours a month you did not expect to spend. Ask directly: do you log in and publish, or do you send me files.

Images and formatting

A Google Doc with no images, no headings styled, and no formatting is a draft, not a finished post. Someone has to lay it out. Clarify whether the service includes sourcing images, adding alt text, and formatting inside your site's editor.

Title tags and meta descriptions

These are the two fields that control how your page looks in search results. They are small, they are fiddly, and they are frequently skipped. A service that writes the body but leaves these blank has handed you the part most business owners do not know how to do well.

Internal links to existing pages

Adding a link inside a new post is easy. Going back into older posts and your service pages to link to the new one is the part that builds structure, and it is almost always the part that gets dropped. Ask whether linking is one-directional or whether they maintain the links across the whole site over time.

What does 'done for you' actually mean in a content service?

'Done for you' means the provider handles every step from topic selection to a published, optimised page on your live website, with no production task left for you except approval. A true done-for-you service requires only your review and sign-off. If you find yourself formatting, uploading, writing meta descriptions, or coordinating with your developer, the service is partially done-with-you and should be priced and described that way. The honest test is simple: after you approve a piece, how many actions do you personally have to take before it is live and working. The answer should be zero or close to it.

A worked example: comparing two providers

Suppose a dental practice in Columbus is choosing between two services, both advertised as "done for you" at a similar monthly price. Here is how they look once you map them against the eight components.

Planned scheduleYes, visible calendarAd hoc delivery
Writing in your voiceYes, after intake callGeneric drafts
EditingSeparate editor passWriter self-edits
On-page optimisationIncludedNot included
Internal linkingMaintained site-wideLinks in new post only
PublishingProvider publishesSends Google Doc
ReportingMonthly summaryNone

On price they look identical. In practice, Provider B leaves the practice manager with topic selection, meta descriptions, formatting, publishing, and performance tracking. That is several hours of skilled work each month, plus the parts the manager is not equipped to do well. Provider A costs the same and absorbs all of it. The advertised price was never the real comparison.

How do I check what's included before I sign?

Ask the provider to walk you through exactly what happens from the moment a topic is chosen to the moment the post is live, step by step. A complete service can answer this without hesitation because the steps are their actual process. Watch for vague answers around publishing and optimisation, since those are the stages most often skipped. Then ask three specific questions: who logs into my site to publish, who writes the title tag and meta description, and who adds links from my existing pages to the new one. If any answer is "you do" or "your developer does," you now know the real scope, and you can decide whether the price still makes sense.

A short checklist to take into the sales call

  • Do you publish directly to my site, or send me files?
  • Are title tags and meta descriptions written for every piece?
  • Do you add and maintain internal links across my whole site, not just inside new posts?
  • Do you handle images and formatting inside my CMS?
  • Can I see the schedule in advance?
  • Is there a separate editing pass, or does the writer self-review?
  • What report do I get, and how often?

Print it, read it down the line during the call, and note where the answer moves work back to you. The total of those shifted tasks is the hidden cost that the headline price hides.

The practical takeaway: "done for you" is not a feature, it is a scope. Before you sign anything, make the provider name the exact point where their work stops and yours begins. If that line sits anywhere before the published, optimised, linked page on your live site, you are buying a draft service at a done-for-you price.
