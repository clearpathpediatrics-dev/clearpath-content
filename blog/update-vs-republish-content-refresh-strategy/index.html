When to Update vs Republish an Old Blog Post

August 9, 2026 · 7 min read

content refreshcontent strategyorganic visibilitycontent updateson-page SEO

Most "refresh your old content" advice stops at "go update your posts." That's not a strategy, it's a chore. Update and republish are two separate moves that solve two separate problems, and picking the wrong one either wastes an afternoon or quietly hurts a page that was fine.

Here's the distinction that matters. An update changes the content on a page — you rewrite sections, add new information, fix outdated claims. A republish changes the visible publish date and often bumps the post back to the top of your blog feed, sometimes with a new URL. You can do one without the other. Doing both, on the wrong page, is how people accidentally tank rankings they didn't need to touch.

This piece walks through how to decide, page by page.

Start with the diagnosis, not the fix

Before you touch anything, figure out what's actually wrong with the page. Pull three data points for each candidate post:

  • Traffic trend over 12 months — flat, declining, or dead from the start.
  • Current ranking position for its main query — page one but slipping, stuck on page two, or nowhere.
  • Whether the information is still accurate — pricing, dates, product names, steps that changed.

Those three answers tell you which of four moves applies: update, republish, consolidate, or leave it alone. Most people skip straight to "rewrite everything," which is the most expensive option and often the wrong one.

When to update (change the content)

Update when the page still ranks reasonably well but the information has aged, or when it's stuck just outside where it should be and the content is genuinely thinner than what's ranking above it.

Signs you need an update:

  • The post ranks position 5-15 for a query with real search demand, and the pages above it simply cover more.
  • Facts, figures, or steps in the post are wrong now. A 2021 pricing guide with 2021 prices is actively misleading.
  • The search intent shifted. A query that used to want a definition now wants a comparison or a how-to, and your post answers the old question.

An update means real content work: adding sections that answer follow-up questions, cutting padding, tightening the intro, adding a table or checklist where a wall of text used to be. You are trying to make the page a better answer than it was.

Here's the part people get wrong: an update does not require changing the publish date. If a post from 2022 is still accurate and you're adding one new section, edit it in place, leave the date, and move on. Search engines re-crawl edited pages regardless of the visible date.

When to republish (change the date and resurface it)

Republish when the content is already strong or freshly updated and freshness is a genuine ranking or trust factor for that topic. Some queries reward recency — anything with a year in it, anything tied to current pricing, tools, regulations, or "best of" lists. A reader landing on a guide dated three years ago will bounce even if the advice is timeless.

Republish makes sense when:

  • You did a substantial update and want the visible date to reflect it, so readers trust it's current.
  • The topic is time-sensitive and a stale date is costing you clicks even when you rank.
  • You want the post back in your feed and newsletter because it's evergreen and still useful to your audience.

The mistake here is republishing thin or unchanged content just to game a date. Bumping the date on a post you didn't meaningfully improve does nothing for rankings and trains you to confuse activity with progress. Change the date because the content changed, not the other way around.

A word on URLs

Do not change the URL during a republish unless you have a strong reason. If you must — say the old slug had a year in it — set up a 301 redirect from the old URL to the new one. Skipping the redirect throws away every link and every bit of ranking the old URL earned. This single mistake undoes more refresh work than anything else on this list.

Should I update or write a new post instead?

Update the existing post when it targets the same core query you'd be writing about anyway — a new post would just compete with your old one and split your own authority. Write a new post only when the topic is genuinely distinct or when the old post covers a different angle you want to keep. Two pages chasing the same keyword is called keyword cannibalization, and it usually means neither ranks as well as one strong page would. When you find two of your own pages competing, the better move is to consolidate: merge the weaker into the stronger, redirect the old URL, and keep one authoritative page.

How often should I refresh old blog content?

Refresh on a trigger, not a calendar — audit your library twice a year, but only act on pages that show a real signal, like a traffic decline, a slipping ranking, or information that's gone out of date. Refreshing everything on a fixed schedule wastes time on pages that are fine and ignores the ones quietly dying between reviews. A practical rhythm for a small business: a full content audit every six months, a quick check on your top 10 traffic pages every quarter, and an immediate update any time a fact in a published post becomes wrong.

A worked example

Suppose a dental practice in Columbus has three aging posts. Here's how the decision plays out for each.

PostSituationMove
"Cost of Invisalign" (2021)Ranks position 6, prices in the post are three years oldUpdate the numbers, add a range table, then republish — pricing content needs a current date
"How to floss properly"Ranks position 4, still accurate, steady trafficLeave it alone — no signal, no action
"Teeth whitening options" and "Types of teeth whitening"Two posts, same query, both stuck on page twoConsolidate into one, 301 the weaker URL to the stronger

Three posts, three different answers. That's the whole point — "refresh your content" without diagnosis treats all three the same and gets one right by accident.

The refresh checklist

Run each declining page through this in order:

  1. Is the information still accurate? If no, update it. This is non-negotiable regardless of traffic.
  2. Does another page of yours target the same query? If yes, consolidate before doing anything else.
  3. Does it rank 5-15 with real demand above it? If yes, expand and improve the content.
  4. Did you make substantial changes, and is freshness relevant to the topic? If yes to both, republish with a new date.
  5. Did you change the URL? If yes, add a 301 redirect immediately.
  6. None of the above? Leave it alone and spend the time on a page that needs it.

The takeaway

Update fixes weak or outdated content. Republish signals freshness on content that's already strong. They're not interchangeable, and neither is a substitute for a diagnosis. Before you refresh anything, pull the traffic trend, the ranking, and the accuracy check — then let those three answers pick the move. Most of your library won't need touching, which is the good news: it means the pages that do need work get your full attention instead of a rushed, library-wide date bump that accomplishes nothing.
