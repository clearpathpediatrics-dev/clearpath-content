When to Kill a Content Program That Isn't Working

October 10, 2026 · 7 min read

content ROIcontent marketing measurementcontent strategymarketing budgetSEO performance

Nobody sets up a content program expecting to shut it down. But a lot of them should be shut down, and the people paying for them usually know it months before they act. The problem is that content has a long feedback loop, so it is hard to separate "this is not working" from "this has not worked yet." Those are different situations with different answers.

This piece gives you a way to tell them apart, a review you can run in an afternoon, and a decision rule you can actually commit to. It assumes you are spending somewhere between $200 and $500 a month, or the internal equivalent in staff time, and you want to know whether to cut it.

The two ways content programs fail

There are only two real failure modes, and they look almost identical on a traffic chart.

Failure of patience. The program is built correctly, the articles are good, they are indexed and interlinked, and they are slowly gaining ground. But the owner expected results in three months, it has been five, and the revenue line has not moved. This program does not need killing. It needs time, and maybe a tighter focus on commercial topics.

Failure of mechanism. The program is producing content that cannot work no matter how long you wait. Thin articles, topics nobody searches for, pages that never get indexed, no internal linking, writing that does not match what the business actually sells. More months will not fix this. More months will just cost more money.

Before you decide anything, you have to know which one you are looking at. A traffic chart alone cannot tell you. You have to open the hood.

The 45-minute diagnostic before any kill decision

Run this in order. You need Google Search Console, Google Analytics (or your analytics tool), and the list of articles the program has published.

  1. Indexation. In Search Console, check how many of your published pages are actually indexed. If you published 40 articles and 12 are indexed, the program has a technical or quality problem, not a time problem.
  2. Impressions trend. Look at impressions over the last six months, not clicks. Rising impressions on a flat click line means you are ranking but on page two or three, which is normal early progress. Flat impressions after six months means the content is not being seen as relevant to anything.
  3. Query relevance. Pull the queries your articles show up for. Are they commercial and related to what you sell, or are they random informational terms with no buying intent? A dentist ranking for "why do teeth hurt" with no path toward "book a cleaning" has a topic-selection problem.
  4. Page quality spot check. Read five articles as if you were a prospect. Would they make you trust the business? Do they answer a real question? Thin, generic, clearly-templated content rarely recovers.
  5. Internal linking. Check whether articles link to each other and to your money pages. An orphaned pile of blog posts with no links between them is a structural failure.
  6. Conversion path. For any article getting traffic, is there a way for a reader to become a lead? A call button, a form, a relevant next step. Traffic with no path converts at close to zero and always will.

If most of these check out and the problem is just that revenue has not arrived yet, you have a patience situation. If three or more are broken, you have a mechanism situation.

How long should you give a content program before shutting it down?

Give a correctly-built content program at least 9 to 12 months before judging it on revenue, but judge its mechanics far sooner, at the 90-day mark. The distinction matters. Revenue from organic content lags because rankings build gradually and buying cycles take time, especially in B2B and high-consideration services like law or home renovation. Expecting pipeline at month three is the single most common reason good programs get killed early.

But mechanics do not lag. By 90 days you should already see pages indexed, impressions climbing, and articles ranking for something, even if that something is on page two. If those early signals are flat at 90 days, the program will not suddenly become effective at month ten. You can and should intervene then without waiting a full year to watch it fail slowly.

A practical schedule: check mechanics at 90 days, check leading indicators (keyword positions, impressions, assisted conversions) at six months, and make the revenue call at 9 to 12 months.

A worked example

Suppose a commercial HVAC company has been running a content program for seven months at $349 a month, roughly $2,400 spent. The owner sees no new jobs attributed to the blog and wants to cut it.

The diagnostic shows: 31 of 34 articles indexed, impressions up from near zero to about 9,000 a month, and the site now ranks in positions 6 to 14 for terms like "commercial rooftop unit replacement cost" and "emergency HVAC service [city]." Two articles have driven three contact-form fills, one of which became a quote.

This is not a failing program. It is a working program in month seven, which is early for commercial HVAC. The mechanics are sound, the topics are commercial, and it is climbing toward page one on terms that signal buying intent. Killing it here would mean paying for seven months of groundwork and then walking away right before the rankings mature. The right move is to keep going and push the near-page-one articles harder.

Now flip it. Same spend, same seven months, but only 12 of 34 indexed, flat impressions, rankings for things like "history of air conditioning," and zero conversion paths on any page. That program is broken at the mechanism level. Either rebuild it with a completely different topic and structure approach, or shut it down. Giving it five more months changes nothing.

The decision rule

Use this simple test. A content program earns continuation if it meets both of these:

  • The mechanics are sound — pages indexed, impressions rising, rankings on commercial terms, working conversion paths.
  • The trend is up and to the right — even slowly. Six months of genuine improvement beats a flat line every time.

Shut it down, or rebuild from scratch, if after honest review the topics have no commercial relevance, pages are not being indexed or read, there is no conversion path, and there has been no upward movement in impressions or positions over a sustained period. At that point you are funding activity, not outcomes.

One caution on the rebuild option. "Rebuild" is not the same as "keep paying the same provider to do the same thing." If the mechanism is broken, the approach has to change: different topic selection, better writing, proper internal linking, and conversion paths added to every page. A good program maps the question-space of your market before writing a word, which is the step most failed programs skipped. This is the kind of structural work a firm like ClearPath Content does, but the point stands regardless of who does it — a rebuild means changing the method, not just extending the contract.

Can you restart a shut-down content program later?

Yes, and the published articles usually keep their value even after you pause, as long as the pages stay live. Content does not expire the moment you stop paying. Pages that were indexed and ranking will generally hold their positions for a while, and a few will keep gaining. So shutting down a program is rarely a total write-off if the work was sound — you keep the asset and stop the spend.

The restart risk is different. If you let pages go stale for a year in a fast-moving field, or you take the content offline entirely, you lose the accumulated authority and have to rebuild it. If you simply pause production but keep the published library online and interlinked, restarting later is far cheaper than starting from zero. Keep the pages up even if you stop writing new ones.

The practical takeaway

Do not kill a content program because the revenue is late. Kill it because the mechanics are broken and the trend is flat after an honest look. Run the 45-minute diagnostic before you make the call, separate failure of patience from failure of mechanism, and give sound programs the 9 to 12 months they actually need. If the mechanism is broken, do not extend the contract — change the method or stop. And whatever you decide, keep the published pages live, because the asset outlasts the subscription.
