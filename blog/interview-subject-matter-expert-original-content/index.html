How to Interview Your Expert for Original Content

September 21, 2026 · 7 min read

subject matter expert intervieworiginal contentcontent briefingSME contenteditorial process

Most content fails for the same reason: it repeats what's already ranking. An article that restates the top five results adds nothing a reader or an answer engine can't get elsewhere. The thing that separates content worth publishing is original insight — a specific number, a counterintuitive judgment call, a mistake the writer watched a client make. And the fastest place to find that is the expert already inside your business.

The problem is that experts are terrible at handing this over on request. Ask a 20-year HVAC installer "what should people know about ductwork" and you'll get a shrug and something you could have found on any manufacturer's site. The insight is in their head, but it's stored as instinct, not as sentences. Your job in a briefing is to convert instinct into quotable, specific material a writer can build around.

Why a bad brief wastes the expert's time and yours

An expert interview goes wrong when you treat it like a survey. Broad questions get broad answers. "Tell me about your process" produces a summary, and summaries are exactly what already exists online. You end up with a transcript full of correct, useless statements — the kind of material that makes a draft read like every competitor's page.

The expert also has a limited tolerance for this. A partner at a law firm will give you 30 minutes, maybe 45. If you spend the first 20 warming up with questions that produce nothing, you've burned the budget before reaching anything original. The brief exists to protect that window.

Build the brief before you talk to anyone

The interview brief is not the article brief. It's a separate document you build by doing the research the expert shouldn't have to do. The point is to walk in already knowing what everyone else says, so every question you ask targets the gap.

Step one: map the existing answer

Pull the top eight to ten results for your target topic. Read them and write a single paragraph summarizing the consensus — the points that appear everywhere. This paragraph is your "do not ask" list. If the expert only confirms these, you have nothing.

Step two: find the disagreements and gaps

Note where the existing content hedges, contradicts itself, or goes vague. Those are the seams. Suppose you're writing about tankless water heaters and every article says "they save money over time" without saying how long. That vagueness is a question: "At what point does a tankless unit actually pay back the install cost, and what changes that math?" You've just found something the expert can answer concretely and nobody else has.

Step three: write questions that force specificity

Rewrite each gap as a question that can't be answered in the abstract. Compare these two:

  • Weak: "How do you approach pricing?"
  • Strong: "Walk me through the last quote you sent that a customer pushed back on. What was the number, and what did you say?"

The second question pulls a real event out of memory, and real events contain numbers, objections, and reasoning — the raw material of original content.

What questions actually produce original insight?

The questions that produce original insight ask for a specific past event, a strong opinion the expert is willing to defend, or a number they know from experience that outsiders can only guess at. These three categories reliably break the surface. Everything else tends to return the consensus you already documented.

Use these framings:

  • The last time: "Tell me about the last time a job went sideways. What happened and what would you do differently?"
  • The number nobody knows: "What does this actually cost, including the parts people forget to budget for?"
  • The disagreement: "What's advice that gets repeated in your industry that you think is wrong?"
  • The tell: "When a customer says X, what do you already know is coming?"
  • The shortcut and its cost: "Where do competitors cut corners, and how does a customer find out later?"

Each of these anchors the expert to a concrete situation. Concrete situations produce quotes. Quotes are what a writer turns into a paragraph no competitor can copy, because it happened to your business and not theirs.

Run the interview so you capture the good parts

Record everything, with permission, and transcribe it. Do not try to take notes and listen at the same time — you'll miss the follow-up moment where the real insight lives. The best material almost never comes from the planned question. It comes from the offhand remark after it, when you ask "wait, why?"

Here's a workable sequence for a 40-minute session:

  1. Minutes 0–5: Explain what the article is for and who reads it. Give the expert permission to be blunt and to say "most people get this wrong."
  2. Minutes 5–30: Work through your gap questions. When an answer gets specific, stop and dig. "You said about $1,200 — is that typical, or was that job unusual?"
  3. Minutes 30–37: Ask the catch-all: "What do customers ask you that they should be asking, and what do they never ask that they should?"
  4. Minutes 37–40: Read back the two or three strongest points and confirm you've got them right. This protects you from publishing something the expert didn't mean.

Follow the energy. When an expert's pace picks up or they say "honestly," you're near something they care about. That's usually where the original insight sits.

Turn the transcript into something usable

A raw transcript is not a draft. Go through it and mark every passage that contains one of four things: a specific number, a named mistake, a strong opinion, or a step-by-step process. Everything else is context. Those marked passages become the spine of the article; the writer's job is to connect them, verify them, and put them in the client's voice.

Send anything numeric or legally sensitive back to the expert for a factual check before publishing. This is the step that separates a business willing to stand behind its content from one that publishes whatever the transcript happened to contain.

A quick before-and-after

Suppose a dental practice wants a page on whether to repair or replace a cracked crown. The consensus content says "it depends on the severity." Your interview with the dentist produces this: "If the crack is above the gumline and the tooth underneath is solid, I repair it — that's a $200 fix versus $1,400. Below the gumline, replacing is the only honest option, because a repair fails within a year and the patient pays twice." That single answer, with its numbers and its judgment, outperforms the entire vague consensus, and no competitor has it.

This is the whole reason the interview exists. At ClearPath Content, the expert interview is the step we protect hardest, because it's the one input a competitor genuinely cannot reproduce.

The takeaway

Do the reading before the interview so every question targets a gap, ask for specific past events rather than general opinions, record and mine for numbers and judgment calls, and fact-check anything concrete before it goes live. The expert already has the original insight. A good brief is just the mechanism that gets it out of their head and onto a page that can rank.
