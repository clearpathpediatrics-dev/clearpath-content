Why a Competitor 2 Miles Away Outranks You Locally

October 28, 2026 · 7 min read

local searchmap pack rankinggoogle business profilelocal seoproximity

You know the situation. You search your main service plus your city, and a competitor two miles up the road sits above you in the map pack. Their office is smaller. Their trucks are older. You've been in business longer. And yet Google keeps putting them first.

This is one of the most common frustrations in local search, and the explanation is almost never "they paid Google" or "the algorithm is broken." Google is measuring a specific set of signals, and your competitor is winning on a few of them. Once you know which ones, the gap stops feeling mysterious and starts looking like a punch list.

The three things Google weighs for local results

Google's own documentation describes local ranking as a mix of three factors: relevance, distance, and prominence. That framing is simple, but each one hides a lot of mechanics.

Relevance is how well your profile and website match what the person searched. If someone types "emergency plumber" and your Google Business Profile category is set to "Plumber" with no emergency service listed, you're less relevant than the shop that spelled it out.

Distance is how far each candidate business is from the searcher — or from the city center when the search has no obvious location attached. This is the one you can't change, and it's often the real reason a nearby competitor edges you out for certain users.

Prominence is how well-known and trusted the business appears to be, based on review volume, review quality, links, citations, and the overall strength of the web presence. This is where most of the fixable gap lives.

Why does a closer business rank higher even when I'm better?

A closer business ranks higher because distance is a genuine ranking factor, and for many searches it carries real weight. When someone searches without naming a neighborhood — just "roof repair" on their phone — Google uses their actual location to decide which results are most useful. If your competitor is two miles closer to that searcher, they get a head start on that particular query, regardless of who does better work.

Two things follow from this. First, you and your competitor may both be "number one" depending on where the searcher is standing. Rankings in local search are personalized by location, so there isn't one fixed leaderboard. Second, distance is not the whole story. If you're clearly more prominent — stronger reviews, a deeper website, more citations — you can outrank a closer business anyway. Google trades these factors off against each other. The nearby competitor beating you usually means they're ahead on prominence or relevance too, and distance is just the tiebreaker you notice.

Run this diagnosis before you change anything

Guessing wastes months. Spend an hour comparing your profile to the competitor's, factor by factor. Open both Google Business Profiles side by side and work down this list.

By the end you'll usually see a clear pattern. Most of the time the closer competitor is ahead on reviews, website depth, or category precision — not on anything you can't match.

A worked example

Suppose you run an HVAC company in Mesa, Arizona. A competitor two miles away outranks you for "AC repair Mesa." You run the diagnosis and find:

Distance is the one line you can't fix. But look at the other four rows — your competitor isn't winning because they're closer. They're winning because their category matches the exact search, they have four times the reviews with fresh momentum, they've listed every service, and their site has a page for each thing they do. Distance is just the margin on top. Close the other four gaps and you can overtake them even from a mile farther out.

The fixes, in priority order

Work top to bottom. Earlier items tend to move the needle faster and cost less.

  1. Fix your primary category. Match it to how people search for your core service, not how you describe yourself internally. This can shift rankings within days.
  2. List every service and product. Fill the Services section completely with the exact terms customers use.
  3. Restart review velocity. A steady trickle of recent reviews beats a big pile of old ones. Ask every satisfied customer, make it one tap, and reply to each review.
  4. Build out your website by service and city. One page per service, one page per city you serve, each genuinely written — not spun duplicates. This is the heaviest lift and the one competitors most often skip.
  5. Clean up citations. Make your name, address, and phone identical everywhere. Fix the old directory listing with your previous suite number.
  6. Add photos regularly. Job-site photos tagged to the areas you work in.

The website build is where the compounding happens. A map pack listing alone has a ceiling; the businesses that pull ahead and stay ahead usually have a web presence deep enough to answer the questions behind dozens of searches, which feeds both relevance and prominence over time. What you can't change, and why that's fine

You can't move your office, and you can't make Google ignore distance. For searchers standing right next to your competitor, you may always sit a notch below them — and that's reasonable, because for that person the closer shop genuinely is more convenient. What you can do is win the wider pool: the searches that name your city, the searches from across your service area, and every query where prominence and relevance outweigh a two-mile gap.

Stop treating the map pack as a single fixed ranking. It's hundreds of different results depending on where each person searches from. Your job isn't to beat one competitor at their own front door. It's to be the most complete, most reviewed, most relevant option across the whole area you actually serve.

Takeaway: Run the side-by-side diagnosis this week. Nine times out of ten, the closer competitor is ahead on category precision, review velocity, or website depth — all of which you can match. Distance is the excuse; the fixable factors are the reason.
