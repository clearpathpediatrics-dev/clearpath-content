How Many Articles Does a Local Business Need to Rank?

October 20, 2026 · 7 min read

local SEOcontent strategyorganic visibilityblog strategykeyword research

The honest answer to "how many articles do I need" is "it depends," which is useless if you're trying to plan a budget. So let's make it useful. The number depends on three things you can actually measure: how competitive your terms are, how much content your competitors already have, and how many distinct questions your market asks. Once you look at those, the range tightens from "somewhere between 5 and 500" to something you can commit to.

This piece walks through how to size a content plan for a local service business before you write a single word. The goal isn't a precise count. It's avoiding the two expensive mistakes: publishing ten posts and quitting because "SEO doesn't work," or committing to 200 articles when 40 would have covered the market.

Why there's no universal number

Rankings are relative. You're not clearing a fixed bar — you're outcompeting the specific pages currently ranking for a specific search in a specific area. A dentist in a small town might rank for "teeth whitening [town]" with a single well-built page because nobody local has written anything. A personal injury firm in a major metro might need dozens of pages covering every accident type, injury, and sub-location just to get noticed, because every competitor has already done it.

So the useful question isn't "how many articles in general." It's "how many does it take to cover the question-space my customers search, and to look as substantial as the sites already ranking." Those two factors — coverage and competitive depth — set the number.

The two forces that set your number

1. The size of your question-space

Every service has a finite set of things people search before they buy. For a residential HVAC company, that includes the money terms ("AC repair [city]"), the diagnostic questions ("why is my AC blowing warm air"), the cost questions ("how much does a new furnace cost"), the comparison questions ("heat pump vs furnace"), and the maintenance questions ("how often to change HVAC filter"). Map them and you'll usually land somewhere between 40 and 120 distinct, worthwhile topics for a single-location service business. That's the ceiling of what's worth writing. You don't need infinity — you need to cover the real questions once, well.

2. How deep your competitors already are

Pull up the top three sites ranking for your main money term. Count their indexed pages that are genuinely about the service (ignore the privacy policy and the careers page). If the top competitors have 15 relevant pages, you're playing a shallow game and can compete with 20 to 30 good ones. If they have 150, you either need comparable depth over time or a sharper angle on an underserved slice of the market.

How many articles does a local business need to rank?

Most single-location local businesses need 25 to 50 well-built pages to become competitive across their core terms, with the exact number driven by competition. In low-competition markets — a specialized trade in a smaller city — 15 to 25 can be enough to rank for the terms that matter. In saturated markets like legal, cosmetic dental, or home remodeling in a big metro, the realistic range is 60 to 100-plus, built over 12 to 24 months. These figures assume the pages are substantial and genuinely answer the search, not 400-word filler. Ten thin posts almost never move anything; thin content is the most common reason owners conclude content "doesn't work."

Note what these numbers include: your core service pages and location pages count toward coverage, not just blog posts. A business with eight strong service pages already needs fewer articles than one starting from a thin homepage-only site.

  1. List your money terms. Write down every service you want to be found for, with your city attached. A plumber might have 8 to 12: drain cleaning, water heater repair, repiping, leak detection, and so on.
  2. Check the top 3 rankers for each. For your three most important money terms, note how many relevant pages each top competitor has. Take the rough average. That's your depth target.
  3. Build the question list. For each service, write down the real questions customers ask — cost, process, comparison, "is it worth it," "how long does it take," emergency variants. Autocomplete and the "people also ask" box are free tools for this. Expect 4 to 10 questions per service.
  4. De-duplicate. Many questions overlap or belong on the same page. Collapse them. You're looking for distinct pages, not distinct keywords.
  5. Add it up and subtract what you have. Total pages needed minus pages you already own equals your gap. That gap, divided by a realistic monthly pace, gives you a timeline.

A worked example

Suppose a plumbing company in Tucson wants to rank. They map 10 money services. The top competitors average about 30 relevant pages, so that's the depth to aim for. The question list, after de-duplicating, produces roughly 35 supporting topics (cost of a water heater replacement, signs of a slab leak, how to find a licensed plumber, hard-water fixes common to the area, and so on). Add 10 service pages plus 3 location-area pages, and the plan lands near 45 to 48 total pages.

They already have a homepage and 4 thin service pages. So the real build is about 40 new or rebuilt pages. At 8 pages a month, that's five months to coverage, then several more months for those pages to mature and earn rankings. A sensible commitment is 12 months, not 3.

Does publishing more articles always help?

No — past the point of covering your question-space well, raw volume stops helping and can hurt. Once you've written the pages people actually search for, additional articles compete with your existing ones, dilute your internal linking, and create maintenance work without adding reach. A site with 40 focused pages that each answer a real query will usually outperform a site with 200 overlapping, thin posts. After you hit coverage, the work shifts from "write more" to "keep accurate, consolidate competing pages, and strengthen the pages that are close to ranking." Volume is a phase, not a permanent strategy.

The exception is markets where the competition keeps publishing. There, you maintain a slower ongoing cadence to hold ground — but you're matching the market, not chasing a number for its own sake.

Cadence matters more than the total

A steady 6 to 8 pages a month beats a burst of 30 followed by silence. Search engines reward sites that update consistently, and your internal linking compounds when new pages connect to older ones. Consistency also protects you from the common failure where a business publishes hard for two months, sees nothing yet, and stops right before the work would have started paying off. Content that's six to twelve months old is typically where the rankings arrive.

This is the main reason to run content as an ongoing program rather than a one-time project — the mechanics reward patience and interlinking. It's also why services like ClearPath Content work on a fixed monthly cadence instead of selling a fixed pile of articles: the schedule is doing as much of the work as the word count.

The practical takeaway

Don't start with a number — start with a map. Count your competitors' relevant pages, list the real questions your customers search, collapse the overlaps, and subtract what you already own. For most single-location businesses that math lands between 25 and 50 pages, built steadily over a year. If it lands at 15, don't pad it. If it lands at 80, don't pretend 10 will do. The right number is whatever covers your market's questions and matches the depth of the sites you're trying to pass — no more, and definitely no less.
