Turn the People Also Ask Box Into a Month of Posts

November 26, 2026 · 7 min read

people also askcontent planningkeyword researchorganic visibilitycontent calendar

The People Also Ask box — the expandable list of questions that appears partway down a Google results page — is one of the few free research tools that shows you the actual phrasing people use. Not a keyword volume estimate. Not a guess from a tool. The literal questions Google has decided are related to what someone just searched. If you run a service business and you're staring at an empty content calendar, this box will fill it.

Here's a method I use to go from a single seed search to roughly a month of planned articles in about an hour. No special software required, though a spreadsheet helps.

Why the PAA box is worth planning around

Three things make it useful. First, every question in it is phrased the way a human would ask it, which matches how people now search and how AI answer engines pull responses. Second, the box expands: click one question and two to four more appear, so a single seed can branch into dozens of related questions. Third, it tells you about intent clustering — Google is showing you which questions it considers neighbors, which is a hint about how to group pages.

The box isn't perfect. It mixes buyer questions with idle-curiosity questions, and it sometimes surfaces questions that are too broad to rank for. Part of the method below is filtering out the noise.

The hour-long harvest, step by step

Suppose you run a commercial HVAC company in Phoenix and you want a month of content. Here's the sequence.

  1. Start with a buyer-intent seed, not a brand term. Search something a prospect would type before they know who you are: "commercial HVAC maintenance cost" or "how often should rooftop units be serviced." Avoid searching your company name.
  2. Open the PAA box and expand every question. Each time you click a question to expand it, Google loads new ones at the bottom. Keep clicking for about five minutes. You'll typically pull 20 to 40 questions from one seed.
  3. Paste each question into a spreadsheet as you go. One question per row. Don't edit them yet — capture the exact wording.
  4. Run two or three more seeds. Try a problem-phrased search ("why is my rooftop unit short cycling"), a decision-phrased search ("replace or repair commercial AC"), and a cost-phrased search. Each pulls a different slice of questions.
  5. Dedupe and tag. You'll now have 60 to 100 rows with overlap. Remove exact duplicates and tag each remaining question by intent: informational, comparison, cost, or local.

That's your raw material. The next part is turning a list into a plan.

Clustering questions into articles instead of writing one post per question

A common mistake is treating every PAA question as its own blog post. That produces thin pages that compete with each other. Group them instead.

Look for three to six questions that a single reader would want answered in one sitting. Those become one article. For the HVAC example, these questions naturally cluster:

  • How often should commercial rooftop units be serviced?
  • What does commercial HVAC maintenance include?
  • What happens if you skip HVAC maintenance?
  • How much does a commercial HVAC maintenance contract cost?

That's one strong article — something like "Commercial Rooftop HVAC Maintenance: Schedule, Scope, and Cost." The four questions become your H2 sections, which means the structure writes itself and each section can be surfaced independently by answer engines.

Do this across your tagged list and you'll usually land on 8 to 12 article concepts from a hundred questions. That's more than a month of content at a once-or-twice-a-week cadence.

A quick rule for splitting versus merging

If two questions would be answered by the same two paragraphs, merge them. If answering one fully would make the other feel buried, split them. Cost questions almost always deserve their own section or their own page because people searching cost are further down the buying path and behave differently than people searching "what is."

How do I know which People Also Ask questions are worth answering?

Prioritize questions that sit close to a buying decision and that you can answer better than a generic national site. A question like "how much does a commercial HVAC maintenance contract cost" is worth answering because the person asking it is comparing providers, and a local company can give specifics a national chain won't. A question like "who invented air conditioning" is not worth your time — it's curiosity traffic that never becomes a customer. Score each question on two axes: how close it is to hiring you, and whether you have a genuine informational edge (local pricing, local code, your own field experience). Write the ones that score high on both first. Push the pure-curiosity questions to the bottom or drop them.

Building the four-week calendar

Once you have your clustered article concepts, sequence them so the month has a shape rather than a random pile. Here's a simple layout for a company publishing weekly.

WeekArticle typeExample from HVAC seed
1Problem/diagnosticWhy Your Rooftop Unit Short Cycles (And What It Costs to Ignore)
2Comparison/decisionRepair or Replace Commercial AC: How to Decide
3Cost/pricingWhat a Commercial HVAC Maintenance Contract Actually Covers and Costs
4Scope/how-it-worksCommercial Rooftop HVAC Maintenance Schedule by Season

This mix matters. If you publish four cost articles in a row, you've ignored the people earlier in their research. A diagnostic piece in week one catches someone with a symptom; the comparison and cost pieces catch them as they move toward hiring. Interlink them — the diagnostic article should link to the repair-or-replace piece, which should link to the cost piece. That internal path mirrors how a real buyer thinks.

A publishing checklist for each piece

  • Lead with a direct answer to the main question in the first two sentences.
  • Use the exact PAA question wording as at least one H2.
  • Answer every clustered sub-question in its own section.
  • Include one concrete local or experience-based detail a national site can't copy.
  • Add an internal link to the next logical article in the sequence.

Does writing for People Also Ask questions actually help with AI answer engines?

Yes, because the structure that works for PAA — a clear question as a heading followed immediately by a complete, self-contained answer — is the same structure answer engines extract and quote. When you phrase an H2 as the literal question and put the full answer in the first sentence beneath it, you make it easy for any system, Google's snippet, a chatbot, or a voice assistant, to lift that passage cleanly. The reverse is also true: burying the answer three paragraphs down behind throat-clearing makes it hard to extract, so the machine skips you. Writing tight question-and-answer sections isn't a trick for one platform; it's just a format that happens to be readable by both people and software.

Keeping the well from running dry

One seed-harvest session gives you a month. To keep going, re-run the process monthly and watch for new questions appearing in the box — it changes as search behavior shifts and as seasons turn. Keep a running backlog tab in your spreadsheet of questions you haven't written yet. You'll rarely use everything from a single session, so the leftovers seed the following month.

Also mine the PAA boxes that appear on your own ranking pages. Once an article ranks, search its target query and see which related questions Google now shows — those are your best candidates for the next piece, because they're adjacent to something you already rank for.

The takeaway: the People Also Ask box turns a blank calendar into a researched one in about an hour. Harvest the exact questions, cluster them into articles instead of writing one-post-per-question, sequence them from diagnostic to cost so you catch buyers at every stage, and lead each section with a direct answer. Do that once a month and you'll never again start a content plan from a blank page.
