Who Owns the Content Your Agency Writes?

October 7, 2026 · 7 min read

content ownershipcontent licensingagency contractswork for hirecontent marketing contracts

You pay an agency $300 a month to write articles. Two years later you want to switch providers. Can you take the 60 articles they produced with you? Can you edit them? Republish them? Feed them to a new agency to update?

The honest answer is: it depends entirely on what the contract says, and most contracts say almost nothing useful. The ownership clause is usually one buried paragraph written by the agency's lawyer to protect the agency. That is the paragraph that decides whether you have been building an asset or renting one.

This piece covers the specific language those clauses should contain, where the traps are, and a checklist you can run before you sign anything.

The two models you are actually choosing between

Nearly every content agreement is one of two structures, even when the contract never names them plainly.

Work for hire (full assignment). You own the content outright. The moment it is created and paid for, the copyright is yours. You can edit it, delete it, move it to a new domain, hand it to a competitor's agency, or print it on a billboard. The agency keeps nothing except, usually, the right to list you as a client.

License. The agency owns the content and grants you permission to use it under stated terms. A license can be broad or narrow. The dangerous versions are the ones that quietly limit you: the license ends when the subscription ends, or you can publish but not modify, or the content can be resold to others.

For a business building an organic footprint, work for hire is almost always what you want. You are paying to grow a durable asset on a domain you own. A license that evaporates when you cancel means you are renting your own blog.

What a clean ownership clause should actually say

You do not need to write legal language yourself. You need to recognize whether the right concepts are present. A strong clause covers five things:

  1. Assignment of copyright. The contract should state that upon payment, all right, title, and interest in the deliverables transfers to you. The phrase "work made for hire, and to the extent it does not qualify, Contractor hereby assigns all rights" is the standard belt-and-suspenders wording. That second half matters because "work for hire" has a narrow legal definition and does not automatically apply to independent contractors in every situation.
  2. Payment as the trigger. Ownership should transfer when an invoice is paid, not at some vague later date. This protects you if the relationship ends mid-cycle.
  3. Modification rights. State plainly that you may edit, update, repurpose, and create derivative works. Even under full ownership, add this so there is no argument later.
  4. No license-back that constrains you. Agencies often keep a "license to display the work in our portfolio." That is fine. Watch for anything broader, like a right to reuse or resell your content elsewhere.
  5. Third-party assets. Stock images, licensed graphics, purchased data, and fonts may carry their own terms. The contract should disclose what is licensed rather than owned, and confirm those licenses transfer or that you have standing permission to keep using them.

What should ownership look like if the contract uses the word license?

If the contract licenses rather than assigns content, the license must be perpetual, irrevocable, worldwide, and survive termination, or you do not truly control your own published pages. A license that reads "for the duration of the agreement" means the agency can, in theory, demand you take down everything they wrote the day you cancel. Even if they never would, you have handed them leverage over your site.

If an agency insists on a license model, these are the words that make it safe: perpetual (does not expire), irrevocable (cannot be withdrawn), transferable (you can pass it to a buyer if you sell the business), and including the right to modify and create derivative works. Also require that the license is exclusive so the same article is never sold to another company. Without exclusivity, a licensed-content provider can publish near-identical pieces for your direct competitor.

A perpetual exclusive license with modification rights gets you close to ownership in practice. But it is almost always simpler to just ask for assignment.

The AI and training-data clause nobody wrote three years ago

There is now a question worth settling in writing: can the agency use your content, your brand voice samples, or your performance data to train models or build products? Many agencies draft articles with AI assistance, which is fine, but you want clarity on the other direction.

Two things to pin down:

  • Your inputs. If you hand over internal documents, customer questions, proprietary processes, or a style guide, the contract should bar the agency from using that material outside your account.
  • Your outputs. If you own the content, you decide whether it gets fed into training systems. State that the agency gains no rights to use your deliverables as training data or in aggregated products without permission.

This is not paranoia. It is the same reasoning behind the confidentiality clause you already expect. Content and the data around it have reuse value, and silence in the contract defaults to ambiguity.

A worked example: reading the clause before you sign

Suppose a dental practice in Columbus is reviewing a proposal from a content provider. The ownership paragraph reads:

"All content remains the property of Provider. Client is granted a license to use delivered content on Client's website for the term of this Agreement."

Run it against the checklist:

  • Copyright assigned to client? No. Provider keeps it.
  • Perpetual and survives termination? No. "For the term of this Agreement" means the license dies on cancellation.
  • Right to modify? Not stated. Silence here usually means no.
  • Exclusive? Not stated. The same pages could appear for a practice three towns over.

That is a rent-your-own-blog clause. The fix is a two-sentence replacement the practice can propose: "Upon payment of the applicable invoice, Provider assigns to Client all right, title, and interest in the content, including the right to modify and create derivative works. Provider retains the right to display the work in its portfolio."

If the agency agrees, you are set. If they refuse outright, that tells you something about whether you are buying an asset or renting access to one.

Pre-signature clause checklist

  1. Does the contract assign copyright to you on payment, or only license it?
  2. If it licenses, is the license perpetual, irrevocable, exclusive, and transferable?
  3. Can you edit and repurpose the content freely?
  4. Does ownership transfer if you cancel mid-cycle or the agency folds?
  5. Are third-party assets (images, data, fonts) disclosed, and do their licenses carry over?
  6. Is your input material (style guides, internal docs) protected from reuse on other accounts?
  7. Is there an AI or training-data clause covering your deliverables?
  8. Can you take the raw files, not just live pages, when you leave?

That last point is worth stressing. Even with full ownership, ask how you receive the work. You want the source documents and a usable export, not just published URLs you would have to scrape back off your own site.

How do I get my content if my agency shuts down?

You get your content on shutdown only if the contract already granted you ownership and the files sit somewhere you control, so the protection has to be in place before anything goes wrong. A clause cannot be negotiated after an agency disappears. Two practical safeguards: insist the ownership transfers on payment rather than at project end, and require periodic delivery of source files to your own storage, not only to a dashboard the agency hosts. If everything lives on their platform and they vanish, ownership on paper does not help you if you cannot reach the files. Good providers, including how we structure things at ClearPath Content, keep the published pages on your domain and hand over the underlying documents so there is nothing to lose if the relationship ends.

The takeaway is simple. Before you sign any content agreement, find the ownership paragraph and read it against the eight-point checklist above. If it assigns copyright on payment, grants modification rights, and lets you walk away with the files, you are building an asset you control. If it only licenses content for the term, you are renting, and you should price the deal and your exit accordingly.
