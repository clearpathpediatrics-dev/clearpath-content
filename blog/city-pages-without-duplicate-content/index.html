How to Write City Pages Without Duplicate Content

November 2, 2026 · 7 min read

local SEOcity service pagesduplicate contentlocal search visibilityservice area pages

Every multi-city business hits the same wall. You serve 30 towns, you want a page for each one, and writing 30 genuinely different pages sounds like a month of work you don't have. So the temptation is to write one good page, swap the city name, and ship 29 copies. That's the exact move that gets a whole folder of pages treated as thin, interchangeable, and not worth ranking.

The fix isn't a better spinner or a smarter template token. It's understanding what makes a city page actually about a city, then building a repeatable way to produce that difference without starting from scratch every time. This piece walks through the mechanics.

Why swapped-name city pages fail

When you take one page and change only the city name, you produce what's called near-duplicate content. The pages share 90%+ of their words, their structure, their images, and their internal links. Search engines are good at spotting this. The usual outcome isn't a penalty in the dramatic sense — it's quieter and worse. The engine picks one page it considers canonical, ranks that one occasionally, and ignores the rest. You did 30 pages of work and got one page of value.

There's a second problem that matters more now that AI answer engines pull from pages directly. A model summarising "best HVAC repair in Mesa" has no reason to quote a page that says nothing specific about Mesa. If your Mesa page would read identically with "Chandler" pasted in, it carries no local information worth surfacing. Specificity is the whole game.

What actually needs to change between city pages

Think of a city page as having a fixed spine and variable muscle. The spine — what your service is, how it works, your guarantee, your process — stays consistent, because lying about your process in one city to make the page "different" is absurd. The variable parts are where real local detail lives:

  • Service area specifics: neighbourhoods, nearby towns you also cover from that base, drive times, the ZIP codes you actually reach.
  • Local conditions: the things that make the job different there. Hard water, clay soil, salt air, older housing stock, a specific permit office, a regional climate quirk.
  • Local proof: a real job you did in that area, a named landmark near a project, a review from a customer in that city, photos from local work.
  • Local logistics: response times to that area, whether you charge a trip fee, which branch serves it, local phone number if you have one.
  • Local questions: what people in that specific market actually ask, which can differ by region.

If you fill those five buckets honestly for each city, the pages diverge naturally. You're not trying to be different for its own sake — you're writing down facts that happen to be unique to each place.

The 60/40 rule that keeps it honest

A practical target: roughly 40% of the page should be city-specific material, 60% shared spine. You don't need to measure this to the percentage point. The test is simpler — read the page and ask, "Could I paste a different city name in and have this still be true and useful?" If most of the page survives that swap unchanged, you haven't written a city page. You've written a template with a variable in it.

A worked example: an HVAC company across three cities

Suppose a company runs HVAC service out of Tucson and covers Tucson, Oro Valley, and Marana. The shared spine — how they diagnose a failing compressor, their 2-year labour warranty, their financing — is identical across all three. Here's how the variable muscle changes:

Housing noteMix of older midtown homes with aging ductworkNewer builds, larger homes, zoned systems commonRapid new construction, builder-grade units failing early

None of that is invented filler. It's the kind of thing a technician who actually works those areas already knows. The job is getting it out of their head and onto the page. Three pages built this way share a warranty paragraph and a process section, but a reader — or an AI model — can tell instantly which city each one is about.

How do I write 30 city pages without it taking months?

Build a structured intake once, then fill it per city, because the bottleneck is information-gathering, not writing. Create a short form with the five variable buckets above and fill one row per city. Half of that can come from a 30-minute conversation with whoever dispatches your jobs — they know which areas are slow to reach, what breaks where, and which neighbourhoods you actually serve. Pull ZIP and neighbourhood data from your own service records. Once the intake is complete, the writing goes fast because you're assembling known facts into a fixed structure rather than inventing a page from nothing.

A realistic sequence:

  1. Write the shared spine once, cleanly, as your best single service page.
  2. Build the intake grid and fill it for every city, pulling from dispatch, job records, and reviews.
  3. For each city, draft the local intro, local conditions section, and local proof block — the parts that must be unique.
  4. Assemble: local intro, shared process, local conditions, local proof, local FAQ, local CTA.
  5. Read each finished page against the swap test before publishing.

Batching matters. Doing all 30 intros, then all 30 conditions sections, is faster and more consistent than finishing one page end-to-end before starting the next.

Does Google penalise similar city pages?

There's no specific penalty for similar city pages, but there is a real cost: pages the engine considers near-duplicates get filtered so only one appears, which wastes the effort you spent on the rest. The distinction matters because people hear "penalty" and imagine a manual action. What actually happens is more mundane and more common — duplicate-ish pages compete with each other, dilute relevance signals, and the engine quietly consolidates ranking to one URL. Avoid it by making each page carry information the others don't, not by adding padding or reordering sentences. Reworded sameness is still sameness.

The scaling trap to avoid

Don't create city pages for places you don't actually serve, hoping to catch searches. A page titled "AC Repair in Flagstaff" when you can't realistically get to Flagstaff is a bad bet twice over — it has nothing true to say, so it stays thin, and it generates leads you can't fulfil. The honest version of local scaling is: a page for every place you genuinely cover, with real detail, and no page for places you don't. That constraint is a feature. It caps your page count at the number of pages you can actually make distinct.

This is the kind of structured, per-city build that programs like ClearPath Content are set up to run — mapping the real service area, gathering local detail, and producing pages that read as individual rather than cloned. But the method stands on its own whether you run it in-house or not.

Takeaway: a city page earns its place when it would be wrong if you pasted a different city's name into it. Build one strong spine, gather real local facts for each area through a simple intake, and assemble pages that pass the swap test. If a page survives having its city name replaced, it isn't finished — go find the five things that are only true in that city and put them on the page.
