How to Review a Blog Post When You're Not a Writer

August 27, 2026 · 7 min read

content reviewediting checklistcontent operationscontent quality controlreviewing agency content

You hired someone to write your content because writing isn't your job. Now a draft is sitting in your inbox with a note that says "ready for your review," and you're not sure what you're supposed to be looking for. So you read it, it sounds fine, you reply "looks good," and you move on.

That's how bad content gets published. Not because it's obviously bad, but because nobody reviewing it knew what a review was supposed to catch. The good news: you don't need to be a writer to review content well. You need to check the things a writer can't verify from the outside — the facts about your business, your market, and your customers — and stop worrying about the things a writer already handled.

This is that checklist. It's built for the person who signs off, not the person who wrote the draft.

What you should actually be checking (and what you shouldn't)

Split your attention into two buckets. The first is things only you know. The writer can't know that your service area stops at the county line, that you dropped a product last year, or that "same-day service" means before noon in your business. These errors are invisible to everyone but you, and they're the ones that make a prospect distrust the whole page.

The second bucket is things anyone can check: spelling, grammar, whether links work, whether the headline matches the content. A competent writer or editor should have handled these before it reached you. If you're finding basic errors in bucket two, that's a signal about your writer, not a task you should be doing yourself every week.

Spend 80% of your review time on bucket one. That's where your judgment is irreplaceable.

The 12-point review checklist

Read the draft once straight through, the way a customer would. Then go back with this list. It's ordered from most damaging to least.

Accuracy and claims

  • Every factual claim about your business is true. Pricing, hours, service area, credentials, years in business, guarantees. Read each one and ask "would I say this to a customer's face?"
  • No promise you can't keep. "We respond within an hour" is a liability if you sometimes don't. Soften anything you can't back up every time.
  • Technical details are correct. If the post explains how a heat pump works or what a contingency fee covers, and it's wrong, it costs you credibility with the exact people who know the difference.
  • No invented statistics. If a number appears — "90% of homeowners..." — ask where it came from. If nobody can name a source, cut it.

Fit and voice

  • It sounds like you, not like a brochure. Read one paragraph out loud. If you'd never say it that way to a customer, flag it.
  • The examples match your real customers. A post aimed at commercial clients that only talks about homeowners is aimed at the wrong person.
  • The advice is something you agree with. You're putting your name on it. If the article recommends an approach you think is wrong, say so.

Usefulness

  • It answers the question in the title. If the headline promises "how much does X cost" and the article never gives a range or explains what drives the price, it failed.
  • A reader gets a real answer without calling you. Content that withholds everything to force a phone call annoys people. The article should be genuinely helpful and then invite contact.
  • The next step is clear. By the end, the reader should know what to do — call, book, read a related piece. One clear action, not five.

Mechanics (quick pass)

  • Links go to the right place and work. Click the two or three that matter, especially any to your own pages.
  • Names, places, and numbers are spelled right. Your city, your product names, partner companies. Autocorrect creates these errors constantly.

A worked example

Suppose you run a dental practice and the draft is titled "How Much Does a Dental Crown Cost in 2025." Here's the review in practice.

Read-through: it flows, sounds professional. Now the checklist. The article says crowns run "$800 to $1,500." Your practice charges $1,100 to $1,900 depending on material. Flag it — either update the range or make clear it's a general national figure, not your pricing. The article mentions "most insurance covers 80%." You know plenty of plans cover 50%. Flag it — that overpromise creates angry patients at the front desk. The post says a crown "takes about two weeks." You offer same-day crowns with in-house milling. Flag it — this is a competitive advantage the article is actively hiding.

The voice is fine, the grammar is clean, the closing invites readers to book a consultation. Three flags, all in bucket one, all things the writer could not have known. That's a fifteen-minute review that just prevented three problems. Send those three notes back and the post is ready.

How long should reviewing a blog post take?

A single blog post should take a non-writer 10 to 20 minutes to review properly. One straight read-through of a 1,200-word article is about five minutes. The checklist pass — focused on the facts only you can verify — adds another five to fifteen depending on how many claims the piece makes. If a review is taking you an hour, either the draft has too many problems and you should push it back to the writer, or you're editing sentences that don't need editing. Your time is best spent on accuracy and fit, not on rewriting prose that already works.

Should I fix errors myself or send them back?

Send them back, with specific notes, rather than rewriting the draft yourself. Two reasons. First, if you edit every draft by hand, you become the bottleneck — the whole point of outsourcing content was to free your time, and you've just re-created the work. Second, corrections you make silently don't teach the writer anything, so the same errors come back next month. A note like "our service area is these four counties, not the whole metro — please fix and remember for future posts" fixes the draft and improves the next ten. The exception is a tiny one-word tweak that's faster to change than to explain; do those inline. Everything structural goes back with a comment.

Build a one-page brief so you catch less

The best way to shorten your reviews is to prevent errors before they're written. Give your writer a standing reference sheet: your exact service area, your pricing ranges and what varies them, your guarantees in precise wording, claims you're legally not allowed to make, product names spelled correctly, and three or four things you never want said. A good content operation — this is the kind of thing we set up at the start of every engagement at ClearPath Content — uses that brief so the same facts don't get relitigated every week. Update it whenever a review turns up a new correction, and your flag count drops over time.

The takeaway

Your job in a content review isn't to be an English teacher. It's to be the one person who knows whether the article is true and whether it sounds like your business. Read it once as a customer, then run the checklist with your attention on accuracy, claims, and fit. Send specific notes back instead of rewriting. Keep a brief so the corrections stick. Do that and you'll approve better content in less time — and stop publishing the small errors that quietly cost you trust.
