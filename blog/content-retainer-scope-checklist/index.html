What a Good Content Retainer Scope Includes

October 8, 2026 · 7 min read

content retainercontent marketing scopecontent agency comparisoneditorial servicesmanaged content

Most content retainer proposals you'll read are built to look generous and stay vague. "8 articles a month, fully managed." That sentence can mean a freelancer running a prompt through an AI tool and pasting the result into WordPress, or it can mean a researched, edited, interlinked piece that actually competes. Same price, wildly different product.

The scope is where you find out which one you're buying. Below is what a complete retainer scope covers, line by line, so you can hold two proposals next to each other and see what's missing. Nothing here is exotic. It's just the work that has to happen for content to earn anything back.

The volume line is the least important number in the contract

Everyone anchors on article count. It's the easiest thing to compare, which is exactly why agencies lead with it. But four well-researched, properly structured pieces will outperform twelve thin ones almost every time, because thin content doesn't rank, doesn't get cited, and doesn't move anyone toward buying.

So read the volume line, then ignore it until you've read everything else. A good scope states volume as a range tied to length and depth, not a flat count. For example: "3-4 pieces per month, 1,200-1,800 words, including original research or first-hand input where the topic requires it." That phrasing tells you they're thinking about fit, not just filling a quota.

What "a piece" should mean

Ask the agency to define one unit of work. A defensible definition includes a target query, an outline approved before writing, a draft, one editing pass, internal links to and from existing pieces, a meta title and description, and formatting for publication. If "a piece" just means a drafted document handed over as a Google Doc, you're buying raw material, not finished work, and the gap is yours to fill.

Research and topic selection: who decides what gets written?

This is the part that separates a content program from a word factory. Someone has to map the questions your market actually types and asks, decide which ones you can realistically win, and sequence them so the site builds authority on a theme instead of scattering one-off posts.

A good scope names this work explicitly. Look for:

  • Topic research and prioritisation — not "we'll write about your industry," but a documented list of target queries with some rationale for order.
  • Search intent matching — a note on whether each topic is informational, comparison, or ready-to-buy, because those get written differently.
  • A refresh allocation — hours or slots set aside to update existing pieces, not only to publish new ones. Older content often returns more per hour than net-new.

If the proposal has no research line at all, you are the research department. That's fine if you want to be. Most owners don't have time, and then the writers guess.

Is editing included, or just writing?

Editing is included in a serious retainer and absent from a cheap one, and it's the difference you'll feel most. A single structural edit per piece catches the problems that kill content: buried answers, missing specifics, a headline that doesn't match the body, claims with no support. Writing produces a draft; editing makes it publishable.

Ask directly whether a second person reviews each piece. In thin operations, the writer is also the only reviewer, which means nobody catches the writer's blind spots. A scope that lists "writing and editorial review" as separate steps is telling you two sets of eyes touch the work. That's worth paying for. Also confirm how many revision rounds you get on your side — "two rounds of client revisions" is standard and keeps a project from stalling in endless feedback.

Publishing, formatting, and the mechanical work nobody mentions

A draft sitting in a shared folder earns nothing. Someone has to get it live, and "live" involves more steps than people expect. A complete scope spells out who handles:

  • Loading the piece into your CMS with correct headings and formatting
  • Meta title and description written to length
  • Internal links added both ways — new piece to old, old pieces to new
  • Image selection or placeholders, if that's in scope
  • Scheduling on an agreed cadence
  • A basic technical check: the URL, the canonical, that the page is indexable

Decide this before you sign. The common failure is an agency that writes well and hands you a document, leaving your team to do an hour of formatting and linking per piece. At four pieces a month that's half a day of someone's time you didn't budget for. Either it's in scope or it's your job — just make sure it's written down.

Reporting: what you actually get to see

You need enough reporting to know whether the program is working, and not so much that you drown in vanity metrics. A reasonable scope includes a monthly summary showing what was published, how existing pieces are trending, and which topics are gaining traction. It should connect to something that matters to the business — leads, calls, form fills — not just impressions.

Be wary of two extremes. A retainer with no reporting line leaves you guessing. A retainer that promises a 20-tab dashboard is usually substituting data volume for judgement. What you want is a short read that tells you what happened, what it means, and what's next.

A worked example: comparing two proposals at the same price

Suppose a dental practice is weighing two retainers, both $399 a month.

Scope itemProposal AProposal B
Volume8 articles4 articles
Topic researchNot mentionedMapped query list, prioritised
EditingNot mentionedSeparate editorial pass
Internal linkingNot mentionedBoth directions, each piece
PublishingDelivered as docsPublished to CMS
RefreshesNone1 older piece updated monthly
ReportingTraffic screenshotMonthly summary tied to enquiries

Proposal A looks like twice the content. Once you account for the work it offloads onto the practice — research, editing, formatting, linking — it's cheaper because it's doing less. Proposal B costs the same and delivers finished, connected work. The practice will publish all four of B's pieces and maybe two of A's, because the other six will sit in a folder waiting for someone who's always too busy.

This is the trap. The bigger number wins the comparison and loses the year.

Questions to ask before you sign

  1. What does one unit of work include, from topic to published page?
  2. Who chooses the topics, and can I see the reasoning?
  3. Does a second person edit each piece?
  4. Who publishes, and does that include internal links and meta data?
  5. How many of these hours go to updating existing content?
  6. What's in the monthly report, and does it connect to leads?
  7. Who owns the content if we leave?

If the answers are specific, you're dealing with someone who runs a real process. If they're vague or keep steering back to the article count, you've learned what you needed to know.

A well-scoped program — the kind we build at ClearPath Content — reads as a sequence of steps with an owner for each. That's not a marketing preference. It's the only way the work actually gets finished and published instead of accumulating in a drive.

The takeaway: ignore the headline volume and read the scope line by line. Research, editing, publishing, linking, refreshes, and reporting are the work. If a proposal doesn't name them, those tasks don't disappear — they land on you, and the price you were comparing was never the real price.
