A One-Hour Content Gap Analysis for Small Business

November 16, 2026 · 7 min read

content gap analysisorganic visibilitysmall business SEOkeyword researchcontent strategy

A content gap analysis sounds like something that needs a $150/month tool and a specialist. It doesn't. The useful version — the one that tells you what to write next week — is a spreadsheet, a list of your competitors, and about an hour of focused attention. The goal isn't a 200-row export you'll never read. It's a short list of questions your buyers are asking that you haven't answered yet, ranked by how close they are to hiring you.

Here's the whole method, start to finish, so you can run it today.

What a content gap analysis actually is

A content gap analysis is the process of listing the questions and topics your market searches for, then marking which ones you cover well, cover badly, or don't cover at all. The "gap" is the set of topics your competitors address and you don't — especially the ones close to a buying decision.

Most people overcomplicate it because the tools overcomplicate it. A keyword tool hands you 4,000 phrases sorted by search volume, and you drown. But a small service business doesn't need 4,000 topics. It needs the 15 to 25 questions that come up before someone books. The manual version finds those faster than the tool does, because you already know your buyers and the tool doesn't.

The hour, broken into four blocks

Set a timer. The time limits matter — they stop you from rabbit-holing on one competitor's blog for 40 minutes.

Block 1 — List your money topics (10 minutes)

Open a spreadsheet. In the first column, list every service you sell and every question a buyer asks before hiring you. Don't think about search volume yet. Think about your sales calls and your inbox.

Suppose you run an HVAC company in Tucson. Your list might start like this:

  • How much does a new AC unit cost to install
  • Heat pump vs. AC in a dry climate
  • How long does AC installation take
  • Signs your compressor is failing
  • Do I need a permit to replace my unit
  • Financing options for a new system
  • How to lower cooling bills in summer

Aim for 20 to 30 lines. These are the topics that, if you ranked for them, would put you in front of someone who's about to spend money. Write them the way a customer would type them, not the way you'd name a service page.

Block 2 — Find your three real competitors (10 minutes)

Not your biggest competitor. Your content competitor — the local or niche business that actually publishes articles and shows up when you search your own topics. Pick one or two of your money topics, search them in a clean browser window (incognito, so your history doesn't skew results), and see who ranks on page one that isn't a directory or a national brand.

Note the three domains that keep appearing. Those are the sites you're measuring against. Add them as column headers in your spreadsheet.

Block 3 — Mark the grid (25 minutes)

This is the core of the work. For each topic in column one, check two things: do you cover it, and do your competitors cover it. Use a simple scale instead of yes/no, because "we have a page about it" and "we rank for it" are different things.

Block 4 — Sort the gaps into three piles (15 minutes)

Now the grid tells a story. Read it against four patterns:

  • Competitors cover it, you don't (0 vs. 2s): your priority list. These are proven buyer questions you're invisible for. The AC cost row is the clearest example — high buyer intent, strong competition, and a zero from you.
  • Nobody covers it well (all 0s or 1s): your opening. The permit question is unclaimed territory. Less competitive to rank for, and genuinely useful.
  • You cover it weakly (your 1 vs. their 2s): your upgrade list. The compressor page exists but loses. Often faster to improve an existing page than to write a new one.
  • You already win: leave it alone. Don't rewrite pages that are working.

The output of the hour is a ranked to-do list: usually five to ten topics worth writing or rewriting, ordered by buyer intent first and competition second.

How do I know which gaps to fill first?

Fill the gaps closest to a buying decision first, even if they have lower search volume. A page on "cost to install a new AC unit" brings in fewer visitors than "how to lower cooling bills," but the cost-page reader is picking a contractor this week and the bills reader might just want a free tip. Rank your gap list by intent: price and cost questions, "vs." comparisons, and "how to choose / who to hire" questions sit at the top. Informational how-tos sit lower. Within the same intent level, prefer the gaps where competitors are weakest, because those are the ones you can rank for without a long fight. This ordering matters more than any volume number a tool shows you.

Do I need a paid keyword tool to do this?

No — for a small service business, a paid keyword tool is optional for a first gap analysis, because your own sales conversations and the live search results give you better buyer-intent signal than volume data does. The free inputs you already have are enough: your inbox, your call notes, Google's autocomplete, the "People also ask" boxes on the results page, and the "related searches" at the bottom. Type a money topic into Google and the autocomplete suggestions alone will surface phrasings you hadn't considered. A paid tool becomes worth it later, when you've already published against the obvious gaps and want to find the long tail — but it's not the thing standing between you and a useful analysis today.

Turning the list into published pages

A gap list is only worth the hour if it becomes writing. The failure mode is a tidy spreadsheet that sits in a folder while the business stays invisible for the same questions next quarter.

Keep the follow-through simple. Take the top three gaps and commit to one article each over the next three weeks. Write each page to answer the question completely and directly in the opening lines, then interlink it to the related service page. When you finish those three, re-run blocks three and four for the remaining topics — it takes 20 minutes the second time because the spreadsheet already exists.

If producing the articles is the bottleneck rather than finding the gaps, that's the part a service like ClearPath Content handles — mapping the question-space and publishing against it on a cadence — but the analysis above is yours to run regardless of who does the writing.

The takeaway

You don't need a tool, a budget, or an agency to know what you should be writing. You need 30 topics your buyers actually ask about, three competitors who publish, and a grid that marks who covers what. An hour of honest scoring produces a ranked list of five to ten pages — the real work is writing them, not finding them. Run the grid this week, pick the top three gaps, and put the first article in front of someone next week.
