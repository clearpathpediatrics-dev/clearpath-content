What's a Good Click-Through Rate in Search?

October 24, 2026 · 7 min read

click-through rateorganic CTRsearch consolecontent measurementseo roi

Click-through rate is one of the few search metrics you can measure directly for your own site without guessing. It is the percentage of people who saw your result in the search listings and clicked it. If 100 people saw your page in the results and 4 clicked, that is a 4% CTR.

The problem is that "good" has no fixed value. A 4% CTR might be excellent for one query and a sign of trouble for another. The number only means something once you account for where you rank, what the person was trying to do, and how much screen space the results page gives you. This piece walks through how to read your own CTR honestly instead of comparing it to a chart you found somewhere.

Why position matters more than any benchmark

CTR falls sharply as you move down the page, and that pattern dwarfs almost every other factor. The result in the top spot gets a large share of clicks. The second and third spots get progressively less. By the time you are at the bottom of the first page, you are competing for a small slice of whatever clicks are left.

This is the first thing to understand before you panic about a low number. A page sitting in position 8 with a 1% CTR is not broken. It is doing roughly what position 8 does. The fix there is not rewriting the title to be catchier — it is climbing to position 3 or 4, where a far larger share of clicks is available.

So the honest version of the question is not "is my CTR good" but "is my CTR good for the position I hold." You judge the result against its own rank, not against a global average.

How to read your actual CTR in Search Console

Google Search Console gives you the real data for your own site, which beats any third-party estimate. Here is the sequence to get a reading you can trust.

  1. Open the Performance report and set the date range to the last three months so you have enough impressions to be stable.
  2. Turn on all four metrics: clicks, impressions, CTR, and average position.
  3. Switch to the Queries tab, then sort by impressions, highest first.
  4. For each high-impression query, look at the CTR next to its average position. That pairing is the only comparison that means anything.
  5. Flag any query where the position is strong — say, 1 through 5 — but the CTR looks weak for that spot. Those are your real opportunities.

A worked example. Suppose a dental practice in Columbus has a page showing these numbers for three queries:

QueryImpressionsAvg. positionCTR
emergency dentist columbus2,4003.19%
cost of dental implants5,8002.42%
how long does a root canal take1,1004.53%

The first query is healthy. Position 3, 9% CTR, clear intent — someone needs a dentist now and your result is pulling its weight. The third query is fine too; it is an informational search at position 4.5, and a 3% CTR there is normal because many people read the snippet and never click.

The middle row is the problem. Position 2.4 is excellent, yet only 2% of people click. That gap — strong rank, weak CTR — tells you something is wrong with how the result presents itself, or that the answer is being read straight off the results page. That is where you spend your time.

What is a good organic click-through rate by position?

A good organic CTR depends almost entirely on position, so the fair way to read it is as a range that declines down the page rather than a single target. As a rough, general pattern that most site owners will recognize in their own data: the very top organic spot often pulls somewhere in the high twenties to low thirties as a percentage for clean commercial queries, the next couple of spots settle into the teens, mid-page results land in the low-to-mid single digits, and anything near the bottom of the first page scrapes along at 1 to 2 percent. Treat those as directional, not precise — your real numbers will shift with your industry, device mix, and how crowded the results page is. The practical rule is simple: compare each query's CTR to what that specific position normally earns, and only worry when you are clearly below it.

Intent changes everything about what counts as normal

Two results can sit at the same position and earn wildly different CTRs because the people searching want different things.

Commercial and local queries

When someone searches "emergency plumber near me" or "bankruptcy attorney austin," they intend to act. They want to call, book, or hire. These queries reward a high CTR because the searcher is hunting for a business to contact, and a strong position tends to convert into clicks and then into calls.

Informational queries

When someone searches "why is my water heater leaking" or "how much does a will cost," they want an answer first. Many read the snippet, learn what they needed, and never click through — especially when the results page shows a featured answer up top. A 3% CTR on an informational query at position 4 is not a failure. The page may still be doing its job by building familiarity and feeding later, higher-intent searches.

This is why you cannot lump all your pages into one CTR average and draw conclusions. Separate commercial intent from informational intent before you judge anything.

Why is my CTR low even though I rank well?

A low CTR at a strong position almost always comes down to one of four things, and you can check all of them in a few minutes. First, something is sitting above you on the page — ads, a featured answer box, a map pack, or shopping results — that pushes your "position 2" far down the visible screen. Your rank is technically high but your visibility is not. Second, your title and description read as generic or do not match what the person searched, so the eye skips past them. Third, your result answers the question so completely in the snippet that people get what they need without clicking, which is common on definition and quick-fact queries. Fourth, a competitor's listing simply looks more relevant or more trustworthy — a clearer title, a review rating, a more specific description. Work through those four in order, because the fix is different for each.

How to improve a weak CTR you can actually fix

Once you have found a query with strong position and weak CTR, and you have ruled out an answer box eating the click, the levers are limited and concrete.

  • Rewrite the title tag to match the search. Put the words people actually type near the front. "Dental Implant Cost in Columbus (2025 Price Guide)" beats "Our Approach to Restorative Dentistry."
  • Write a description that earns the click. The meta description does not affect ranking, but it affects whether people choose you over the result above or below. Say what the page delivers in plain terms.
  • Add specificity. Prices, timeframes, locations, and numbers in the title tend to pull attention because they signal a direct answer.
  • Match the format to the intent. If people want a cost and you make them read three paragraphs of background first, some bounce back to the results and click a competitor. Lead with the answer.

Give any change four to six weeks before you judge it. CTR data is noisy week to week, and you need enough impressions after the change to compare fairly against the period before.

The practical takeaway

Stop asking whether your CTR beats an industry average. Open Search Console, sort your queries by impressions, and look at each CTR next to its average position and its intent. Healthy results at the top of the page with clear commercial intent should pull strong clicks. Informational results and lower positions will not, and that is fine. Spend your effort on the short list of queries where you rank well but barely get clicked — those are the only ones where a better title actually changes the outcome.
