Content Marketing for MSPs That Actually Wins Deals

September 18, 2026 · 8 min read

MSP marketingcontent marketing for MSPsmanaged IT servicesB2B content strategyIT lead generation

Most managed service provider websites have a blog, and most of those blogs are dead weight. They post "5 Cybersecurity Tips for 2024" once a quarter, get no traffic, and generate no calls. The problem isn't that content doesn't work for MSPs. It's that MSPs write for other MSPs instead of for the office manager, controller, or operations director who actually signs the contract.

An MSP sells trust in something the buyer doesn't understand well. That's the whole job of your content: reduce the fear of switching IT providers by showing you already know their situation. This piece covers what to write, how to structure it around how people buy, and how to measure whether it's doing anything.

Write for the person who signs, not the person who fixes

The buyer for managed IT is rarely a technical person. In a 40-person accounting firm, it's the managing partner or the office manager. In a small manufacturer, it's the ops director or the owner. These people don't search "endpoint detection and response comparison." They search things like "how much should IT support cost per employee" or "what to do when your IT guy quits" or "is my company data safe with a former employee."

That gap is your opening. Technical vendors compete over jargon-heavy pages. The plain-language questions sit largely unanswered, or answered badly by generic listicles. If you write the clear, specific answer for a 30-person business owner, you show up where a competitor's datasheet never will.

Two practical moves:

  • Interview your own techs and account managers. Every question a prospect asked during the last five sales calls is an article. "They kept asking whether we'd hold their data hostage" is a real fear and a real topic.
  • Write in the buyer's units. They think in dollars per user per month, downtime hours, and "who do I call at 7am." Not in SLA tiers and stack diagrams.

Map topics to the three stages of an IT buying decision

A business owner shopping for an MSP moves through roughly three phases, and each needs a different type of content.

1. Something is wrong (problem-aware)

They're frustrated, not yet shopping. Trigger events: a security scare, an outage, a slow current provider, an IT employee leaving, a failed audit, a new compliance requirement. Content here names the problem and explains what normal looks like. Examples: "Signs Your Current IT Provider Is Coasting," "What Happens to Your Systems When Your Only IT Person Leaves," "Why Your Wi-Fi Slows Down Every Afternoon."

2. Comparing options (solution-aware)

They now know managed IT exists and want to understand the model and the cost. Content here explains how MSPs work, what pricing structures mean, and how to compare providers without being technical. Examples: "What Managed IT Actually Includes," "Per-User vs Per-Device Pricing Explained," "Questions to Ask Before Signing an MSP Contract."

3. Choosing you (vendor-aware)

They're evaluating specific providers. Content here is proof and specificity: your onboarding process, your response-time commitments, what your first 90 days look like, how you handle a security incident. This is where industry pages matter — a page for "IT support for dental practices" that speaks to HIPAA, imaging software, and practice-management systems beats a generic "we serve all industries" page.

A common mistake is publishing only stage-three content — service pages and "why choose us" copy. That captures people already looking for you by name. The stage-one and stage-two articles are what bring in people who don't know you yet.

How often should an MSP publish content?

Cadence matters less than consistency and topical coverage, but a realistic starting point for most MSPs is two to four solid articles a month aimed at a defined set of buyer questions. One thorough 1,200-word piece that fully answers a real question outperforms four thin 400-word posts. The goal is coverage of a topic area, not raw volume. If you publish four times a month for six months, that's roughly 24 articles — enough to cover the core questions for one industry vertical and the general buying-decision questions. Publishing sporadically, then going quiet for a quarter, is the pattern that produces nothing.

Pick a cadence you can actually hold through your busy season, then plan the topics in advance so you're never staring at a blank page. If you can only sustain two a month, do two a month well.

Pick a vertical and own its language

Generalist MSPs blend together. The fastest way to stand out in content is to go deep on one or two industries you already serve well. Suppose you support a handful of law firms. You already know they care about document management systems, client confidentiality, retention rules, and the fact that a partner bills $400 an hour and cannot afford to lose a morning to a dead server.

Write to that. A page titled "IT Support for Small Law Firms" that names the software they run (their practice management platform, their document system), the compliance concerns they face, and the specific downtime cost of a billing attorney will convert far better than a generic services page. Then build supporting articles around it: "How Long Should It Take to Recover a Lost Legal Document," "What Ethics Rules Say About Storing Client Files in the Cloud."

This is also how you defend against being copied. Any competitor can list "24/7 monitoring." Few will do the work to speak fluently to your vertical's daily reality.

A worked example: turning one sales objection into a content asset

Say your salespeople keep hearing: "We're worried switching providers will cause a big disruption." Here's how to turn that into content that earns traffic and warms leads.

  1. Name the article as the buyer would search it. "How Long Does It Take to Switch IT Providers?" — a real query with clear intent.
  2. Answer the exact question in the first line. Give a range and the factors that move it. Don't bury the answer under three paragraphs of preamble.
  3. Show your process. Lay out your onboarding steps: documentation handoff, access transfer, monitoring setup, first-week priorities. This doubles as proof you've done it before.
  4. Address the fear directly. Explain how you avoid downtime during transition and what the outgoing provider is obligated to hand over.
  5. Add one internal link to your service page and one to a related article ("Questions to Ask Before Signing an MSP Contract").
  6. Give sales the link. Now when a prospect raises the objection, the rep sends the article. It does the reassuring for you.

Repeat this for the ten objections you hear most and you have a content library that maps precisely to how you already sell.

Measure the right things

Traffic is a vanity number if none of it fits your buyer. Track these instead:

  • Assessment or consultation requests that mention a specific article, or that came in on a page you can trace.
  • Which pages assist conversions — often a buyer reads three or four articles before contacting you, and the last-click page gets undeserved credit.
  • Rankings for buyer-intent phrases, not just any phrase. "MSP pricing" matters; "what is a computer" does not.
  • Sales team usage — if reps are sending your articles to prospects, the content is working even before it ranks.

Give it time. Content for a considered B2B purchase like managed IT doesn't produce leads in three weeks. Judge it over two or three quarters, and judge it by whether it's answering real buyer questions and getting used in sales conversations.
