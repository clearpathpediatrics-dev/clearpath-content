Local SEO With No Storefront: A Field Guide

November 18, 2026 · 7 min read

local SEOservice area businessGoogle Business Profilecontractor marketinglocal search visibility

Most local SEO advice assumes you have a storefront customers can walk into. A dentist has a lobby. A restaurant has an address on the door. But if you run an HVAC company, a plumbing outfit, a mobile notary, a tree service, or any business where the work happens at the customer's location, that advice falls apart fast. You do not want strangers showing up at your house or your rented yard, and Google has specific rules for businesses like yours.

The good news: Google has a whole category built for you. The bad news: most service-area businesses set it up wrong, leave money on the table in their service-area settings, and never build the page structure that actually wins nearby towns. Here is the field guide.

Set Up Google Business Profile as a service-area business

A service-area business (SAB) is one that serves customers at their location rather than its own. Google lets you hide your address and list the areas you cover instead. This is the single most important setting, and getting it wrong is the most common mistake we see.

Here is the sequence:

  1. Create or claim your Google Business Profile. You will have to enter a real address to verify — use your actual business address, even if it is your home.
  2. After verification, go into the profile and clear the address field so it does not display publicly. Google will keep it on file for verification but stop showing it.
  3. Add your service areas. Google lets you list up to 20 areas — cities, counties, or postal codes.
  4. Keep your service radius realistic. Listing a town two hours away that you will never drive to does nothing for ranking and can confuse the profile's relevance signals.

If you leave your home address visible and you are not actually open to visitors there, you risk suspension. Hiding it is not a trick — it is the designed behavior for your category.

Pick your service areas like a business, not a wish list

Google weights proximity heavily. A customer searching from the center of your home base will see you more easily than one 40 minutes out, no matter how many areas you list. So order your effort by where you can realistically win: your home city first, then the two or three adjacent towns where you already get jobs. Chasing a metro you have never worked in spreads your signals thin.

Do service-area businesses rank in the map pack without an address?

Yes, service-area businesses can rank in the local map pack without a visible address, but proximity to the searcher still drives most of the result, so you will rank strongest near your verified base and weaker the farther out a searcher is. This is the hard truth nobody tells you: hiding your address does not remove the proximity factor — Google still uses your verified location internally to decide how close you are to each searcher. A plumber verified in east Tucson will show up more often for searches on the east side than the west side, even with the whole city listed as a service area.

What this means in practice: the map pack alone will not carry you across a wide region. To reach towns outside your immediate radius, you need the second half of the strategy — your own website pages built for those specific areas. The map pack covers the ground near you; your website covers everything else.

Build a page for every real service area

This is where businesses that work from job sites win or lose organic traffic. You cannot rank in the map pack across a whole county, but you can rank the regular (non-map) search results for "[service] in [town]" with a dedicated page — if the page is actually useful and not a doorway page.

The wrong way: create 30 near-identical pages where only the town name changes. Google treats these as thin, spammy doorway pages and either ignores or penalizes them.

The right way: make each page genuinely specific to that place. For a hypothetical tree service in Boulder County, a page for "Tree Removal in Longmont" should include:

  • A short note on local conditions — which tree species are common there, local permit requirements, the municipal rules for removal near power lines.
  • Real jobs you have done in or near that town, described without naming the customer.
  • Drive time or coverage detail that proves you actually serve it.
  • A relevant local landmark or neighborhood reference that a copy-paste page would never contain.
  • Specific pricing ranges or factors that apply there, if you can give them honestly.

A town page with 400 words of real, place-specific detail beats a 900-word template with the name swapped in. Build these for the towns where you genuinely want more work, not for every dot on the map.

Earn reviews that name the location

Reviews do double duty for service-area businesses. They feed your map ranking, and when a customer writes "they came out to our place in Erie and fixed the line same day," that review text puts a location keyword on your profile without you touching anything. Ask for reviews after every job, and when you ask, prompt lightly: "If you have a minute, mentioning the town and what we did helps other folks nearby find us." You cannot script a review, but you can tell people what is useful.

Spread the timing. A burst of ten reviews in one day followed by silence looks less natural than a steady trickle. Since your crews finish jobs on a rolling basis anyway, build the ask into your job-close routine.

How should I handle my address if I work out of my home?

If you work out of your home, enter your home address to verify the Google Business Profile, then hide it from public view in the profile settings so it never displays, and list your service areas instead. Your home address stays private and your business still gets verified and ranks. Do not use a PO box or a UPS Store mailbox as your address — Google prohibits virtual mailboxes for Business Profiles, and using one risks suspension. Do not rent a cheap shared office you never visit just to have an address, either; a location you do not staff is against the rules and adds no ranking benefit for an SAB.

The one time you should show an address: if you genuinely have a yard, shop, or office where customers can come to you during stated hours. Then it is a legitimate storefront and you keep it visible.

Keep your signals consistent everywhere

Your business name, phone number, and the fact that you are a service-area business should match across your website, your Google profile, and any directory listings. Conflicting information — a visible address in one directory, hidden in another, a different phone on your old Yelp page — muddies the relevance signals Google uses to trust your profile. Run a quick audit: search your business name and check the first page of results for any listing showing stale or wrong details, then fix or claim each one.

For businesses juggling a dozen service-area pages plus ongoing content, keeping the whole structure interlinked and current is more work than most owners have time for. The practical takeaway: hide your address, list only the areas you truly serve, let the map pack cover the ground near your base, and build one genuinely specific page for each town you want to reach beyond that. Proximity still rules the map, so your website does the long-distance work. Do those two things well and you cover far more territory than a storefront ever could.
