The 4 Search Intent Types and How to Write Each

August 8, 2026 · 7 min read

search intentcontent strategykeyword researchorganic visibilityon-page SEO

Most business blogs fail on one decision made before a single word gets written: what the searcher actually wants when they type the query. You can write a genuinely good 1,800-word article and still lose to a thinner page because your format doesn't match the job the searcher came to do. This is the single most common reason a page gets indexed, sits on page three, and never moves.

Search engines sort queries by intent. So should you. There are four categories, and each one wants a different page shape, a different opening, and a different call to action. Here's how to tell them apart and write for each.

The four intent categories

Every query a person types is doing one of four things. Learn to name them on sight and half your content planning problems disappear.

IntentWhat they wantExample queryBest page type
InformationalAn answer or explanation"why is my furnace short cycling"Article, guide, FAQ
NavigationalA specific site or brand"clearpath content login"Homepage, brand page
CommercialTo compare before buying"best tankless water heater brands"Comparison, review, roundup
TransactionalTo act or hire now"emergency furnace repair tucson"Service page, booking page

The mistake I see weekly: a company writes a helpful informational article and slaps a "call us today" transactional pitch on it, or builds a thin service page and expects it to rank for a question people ask in full sentences. The page ends up serving two masters and satisfying neither.

Informational: answer the question completely, then get out of the way

Informational queries are the biggest bucket and the least commercial. The searcher has a problem or a curiosity. They are not ready to buy. Your job is to be the most complete, clearest answer, because that earns the ranking and the trust that pays off later.

Structure that works:

  • Answer the core question in the first two sentences. Do not warm up.
  • Then expand: causes, steps, exceptions, what to do next.
  • Use subheadings phrased as the follow-up questions the same person will ask.
  • One soft, relevant link to a related service page. Not a hard sell.

Suppose an HVAC company writes "why is my furnace short cycling." The reader is standing in a cold house, not filling out a quote form. Give them the five likely causes ranked by how common they are, tell them which ones are DIY and which need a tech, and only then mention that you handle the ones that need a tech. You've been useful first. That's what makes the next page you rank for convert.

Commercial: compare honestly, including options that aren't you

Commercial-investigation queries sit between learning and buying. The person has decided they need something and is now weighing options. "Best," "vs," "alternatives," "reviews," and "top" are the tell-tale words.

These pages rank when they read like a genuine comparison, not a thinly disguised ad. That means including options that aren't yours and being honest about trade-offs. A page that says "here are the three main approaches, here's who each one suits, and here's where we fit" outperforms "here's why we're the best" almost every time, because the format matches what the searcher expected to find.

How to write a comparison that ranks

  1. Lay out the real choices, including ones you don't sell.
  2. Give each a fair pros-and-cons treatment.
  3. State plainly which type of buyer each option suits.
  4. Position your offering as one honest choice, with the specific situation it's right for.
  5. Link to your transactional page for readers who've decided.

A dental practice writing "clear aligners vs braces" should cover cost, treatment time, and who each works for, including the cases where braces still win. The reader trusts the page that admits a downside. That trust is what carries them to your booking page.

What is transactional search intent and how do you write for it?

Transactional intent means the searcher is ready to act — buy, book, hire, or request a quote — and the page's only job is to remove friction between that intent and the action. Write these pages short and specific. The reader doesn't need a 1,500-word essay; they need to confirm you do the thing, in their area, and know how to reach you.

A transactional page for "emergency furnace repair tucson" should load with the service, the service area, the response window, the phone number, and pricing signals near the top. Cut the history of your company. Cut the general education about furnaces — that belongs on your informational pages, which should link here. Elements that matter:

  • The exact service and location in the H2 and first line.
  • A clear, immediate contact method above the fold.
  • Trust signals: licensing, guarantees, response time, review count.
  • Answers to the two or three objections that stop people booking.
  • No competing links that pull the reader off the action.

What is the difference between commercial and transactional intent?

Commercial intent is comparing before deciding; transactional intent is acting after deciding — and the difference determines whether you write a comparison or a conversion page. Someone searching "best CRM for small law firms" is still weighing options and wants a page that helps them choose. Someone searching "clio pricing" or "book demo" has chosen and wants a page that lets them proceed. Serve a comparison page to the transactional searcher and you slow them down; serve a hard conversion page to the commercial searcher and you scare them off before they trust you. The keyword's modifier usually tells you which one you're looking at: "best," "vs," and "alternatives" signal commercial; "buy," "book," "hire," "near me," and brand-plus-"pricing" signal transactional.

How to check the intent before you write

You don't have to guess. The search results already show you what the engine has decided the intent is. This ten-minute check saves you from writing the wrong page.

  1. Search your target query in a clean browser or incognito window.
  2. Look at the top five results. Are they articles, comparisons, or service pages? That's your format.
  3. Check for a featured snippet or "people also ask" box — those signal informational intent and show you the sub-questions to cover.
  4. Note whether local map results appear. If they do, the intent is at least partly transactional and local.
  5. Read the top result's opening two sentences. Match that job, then beat it on completeness.

If the top results are all comparison articles and you were planning a service page, stop. The engine is telling you no one lands a booking from that query yet. Write the comparison, rank, and route the ready buyers to your service page from there.

A quick worked example

Suppose a B2B software company targets "project management software." The results are dominated by roundups and comparisons — clearly commercial. So the company writes an honest comparison of five tools including two competitors, ranks over months, and inside that article links to a transactional page targeting "[product name] free trial." Two pages, two intents, one funnel. That's the pattern that works, and it's the one most content plans skip because they try to make a single page do both jobs.

The takeaway

Before you write anything, answer one question: what does the searcher want to do right now — learn, find, compare, or act? Then build the page shape that job expects, and confirm it against the live results. Getting this right is boring and mechanical, and it's exactly why a smaller page with the right format beats a bigger one with the wrong one.
