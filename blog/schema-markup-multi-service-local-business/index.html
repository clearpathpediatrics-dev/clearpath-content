Schema Markup for Multi-Service Local Businesses

November 26, 2026 · 7 min read

local schemastructured dataservice schemalocal SEOmulti-service business

Most local businesses sell more than one thing. An HVAC company installs furnaces, repairs air conditioners, and does duct cleaning. A law firm handles estate planning, personal injury, and business formation. A dental practice offers cleanings, implants, and orthodontics. The problem: the schema markup on most of these sites describes the business as a single thing and says nothing useful about the individual services.

That gap matters because search engines and AI answer tools lean on structured data to figure out what you actually do. If your markup says "LocalBusiness" and lists your name, address, and phone, you have told a machine that you exist. You have not told it you install tankless water heaters in a specific city. This piece walks through how to structure schema when you offer several distinct services, with the actual markup patterns that work.

Why one LocalBusiness block isn't enough

A single LocalBusiness or its more specific subtypes (Plumber, Dentist, Attorney, Electrician) is the right foundation. But that one block is built to describe the organization, not its catalog. It has fields for name, address, geo coordinates, hours, phone, and price range. There is no native field inside it that says "we do emergency drain cleaning and here's a description of it."

When you run everything through that one block, every service you offer collapses into a brand name. A search engine parsing your site sees a plumber in Tucson. It does not see a plumber who does slab leak detection, repiping, and sewer camera inspection unless you tell it, separately, in structured form.

The fix is to describe each service as its own entity and connect it back to the business. There are two clean ways to do this, and you can use both.

Option one: the Service type

Schema.org has a Service type designed for exactly this. Each service you offer becomes its own Service object with a name, a description, the area it covers, and a provider property that points back to your business. This is the most direct way to make individual services legible.

Option two: hasOfferCatalog with OfferCatalog

Inside your main LocalBusiness block, you can add a hasOfferCatalog property. That holds an OfferCatalog, which holds a list of offers, each wrapping a Service. This nests everything under the business and is a good fit when you want one page (usually the homepage) to declare the full menu of what you sell.

Neither option is wrong. Use Service objects on individual service pages, and use hasOfferCatalog on the homepage to list the whole set. They reinforce each other.

Suppose a plumbing company in Tucson offers drain cleaning, water heater installation, and leak repair. Here is how the structured data breaks down across the site.

On the homepage, the Plumber block carries the core business facts plus an offer catalog:

  • Business type: Plumber (a subtype of LocalBusiness)
  • Name, address, geo coordinates, phone, hours, URL
  • areaServed: Tucson, AZ and surrounding zip codes
  • hasOfferCatalog listing three services: Drain Cleaning, Water Heater Installation, Leak Repair

On each service page, a standalone Service object:

  • serviceType: "Water Heater Installation"
  • name: a plain-language service name
  • description: 1-2 sentences describing exactly what's included
  • areaServed: the same city/region
  • provider: a reference back to the Plumber business (using its name and URL, or an @id reference if you use linked data)

The @id technique is worth understanding. Give your main business block a stable @id (for example, your homepage URL with a #business fragment). Then every Service object's provider can reference that same @id instead of repeating all the business details. This tells the parser that all these services belong to one entity, not three separate businesses.

How do I mark up services that cover different areas?

Set a distinct areaServed on each individual Service object rather than relying only on the business-level area. Many multi-service businesses have services with different footprints. A roofer might do inspections within 20 miles but full replacements only within 10. An HVAC company might offer emergency repair across a whole metro but installation in a tighter radius. When you put areaServed on the Service object itself, you can scope each one accurately. Use a city name, a state, a GeoCircle with a radius, or a list of postal codes. If every service genuinely covers the same area, you can set it once at the business level and skip repeating it, but when the footprints differ, the Service-level field is where the truth lives.

A checklist for getting multi-service schema right

  1. Pick the most specific business type. Use Plumber, Dentist, Electrician, HVACBusiness, Attorney, or whichever subtype fits, not the generic LocalBusiness, when a specific one exists.
  2. Give the business block a stable @id. This becomes the anchor every service points to.
  3. Create one Service object per distinct service. Not per page section, per actual service a customer would search for.
  4. Write a real description for each. One or two sentences, specific, matching the visible page content. Do not stuff keywords.
  5. Set serviceType and name on every Service. serviceType is the category; name is what you call it.
  6. Point each provider back to the business @id. This keeps everything attached to one entity.
  7. Scope areaServed per service when footprints differ.
  8. Add hasOfferCatalog to the homepage listing all services so the full menu appears in one place.
  9. Keep markup consistent with the page. Schema should describe what a human sees on that page. Marking up a service you don't mention on the page invites problems.
  10. Validate before and after publishing. Use Google's Rich Results Test and the Schema.org validator to catch syntax errors.

Does service schema guarantee rich results or rankings?

No. Service and OfferCatalog markup does not produce a guaranteed rich result the way Review, FAQ, or Product markup sometimes can, and no schema guarantees a ranking. What it does is make your individual services machine-readable, which helps search engines and AI answer tools understand the full scope of what you offer and match you to more specific queries. Think of it as removing ambiguity rather than buying placement. A plumber whose markup clearly states "sewer camera inspection in Tucson" is easier to surface for that exact need than one whose markup says only "plumber." The benefit is correct understanding and broader query coverage, not a flashy listing. Treat any vendor who promises specific rankings from schema alone with suspicion.

Common mistakes that quietly break it

A few patterns show up repeatedly and undercut the whole effort.

  • Duplicate businesses. Creating separate LocalBusiness blocks for each service makes it look like you run three companies at one address. Use one business, many services.
  • Markup that doesn't match the page. If the JSON-LD lists ten services but the page mentions three, you've created a mismatch that search engines distrust.
  • Copy-pasted descriptions. Identical descriptions across services signal low effort and tell the parser nothing distinct.
  • Orphaned Service objects. A Service with no provider reference floats disconnected from your business. Always link it back.
  • Forgetting to update. When you drop or add a service, the schema has to change too. Stale markup describes a business that no longer exists.

If you are producing dedicated pages and articles for each service anyway, the schema work fits naturally alongside them. At ClearPath Content, the service pages we build are structured so each one carries its own Service markup pointing back to a single business entity, which keeps the whole site consistent as the service list grows.

The practical takeaway

One business block, many Service objects, each with a real description and an areaServed, all pointing back to a single business @id. Put a full offer catalog on the homepage, put individual Service markup on individual service pages, and keep all of it matching what's visibly on the page. That structure tells search engines and AI tools exactly what you do and where, instead of leaving them to guess from a single generic label. Start with your three or four highest-value services, validate the markup, and expand from there.
