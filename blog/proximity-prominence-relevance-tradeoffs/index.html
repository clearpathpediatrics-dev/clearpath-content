Local Search Visibility

Why You Rank in One Neighborhood But Not the Next

October 16, 2026 · 7 min read

local search ranking factorsmap packproximity vs relevancelocal SEOGoogle Business Profile

If you've ever searched your own service from home and seen yourself in the top three, then searched the same thing from across town and vanished, you've already met the thing this article is about. Local search doesn't hand out one fixed ranking. It recalculates for every searcher based on where they are, how well-known your business is, and how closely your pages and profile match what they typed.

Google has named these three signals for years: proximity (how close you are to the searcher), prominence (how well-known and trusted your business is), and relevance (how well your business matches the query). The part nobody explains clearly is that these three trade off against each other. You can't max all three for every search. Understanding which one is holding you back in a given situation is the whole game.

What each signal actually measures

Start with plain definitions, because the labels get used loosely.

Proximity is distance between the searcher and your physical location (or service-area center). It's the one you control least. A searcher two blocks from your shop gives you a proximity advantage you did nothing to earn. A searcher 11 miles away hands that advantage to a competitor near them.

Prominence is reputation at scale: review count and velocity, the quality and consistency of citations across directories, links from local sources, brand mentions, and general authority. A 30-year-old firm with 400 reviews has prominence a new entrant can't fake.

Relevance is match quality. Does your Google Business Profile category match the query? Do your website pages describe the exact service and location the person asked about? A roofer with a dedicated "metal roof installation" page is more relevant for that term than one with a single "services" page listing everything.

How the three trade off in practice

Here's the mechanism. For a broad, high-intent local search, Google leans hard on proximity. For a specific or unusual query, it leans on relevance because fewer businesses genuinely match. And across both, prominence acts as a thumb on the scale that decides ties.

Suppose a dentist in Columbus searches "dentist near me" from their office. Proximity dominates — they'll see whoever is physically closest with a decent profile. Now someone searches "pediatric sedation dentist Columbus" from the suburbs. Proximity matters less because only a handful of practices offer that. Relevance (a real page about pediatric sedation, the right profile category) and prominence (reviews mentioning kids and anxiety) now decide it.

This is why you can dominate a generic term near your door and lose a specific term you're actually better at. You optimized for the wrong lever.

The three common tradeoff situations

  • You're relevant but far. You offer exactly what they searched, but a closer, more generic competitor outranks you. Push prominence and specificity hard — you need enough signal strength to overcome the distance gap.
  • You're close but generic. You rank at home and nowhere else. Your profile and pages are too broad. The fix is relevance: service-specific pages, tighter categories, location language.
  • You're relevant and close but invisible. You have both and still lose. That's a prominence problem — thin reviews, inconsistent citations, a weak website. This is the most fixable and most common.

Which signal should I work on first?

Work on relevance first, because it's the cheapest to fix and it widens the range of searches you can compete for at all. Proximity is mostly fixed — you can't move your building, and opening locations is a capital decision, not an SEO tactic. Prominence takes months of reviews, links, and consistency to move. But relevance you can improve in weeks: split your one services page into dedicated pages for each real service, match your Google Business Profile primary category exactly to your money service, and use the actual city and neighborhood names your customers use.

Relevance also changes the math on the other two. When you're genuinely the best match for a query, Google tolerates more distance and forgives a slightly thinner review profile. Being specifically relevant effectively extends your proximity radius for that term.

A worked example

Imagine an HVAC company in Tucson with one location on the east side. Current state: ranks top three for "AC repair Tucson" within about three miles, invisible beyond that, and absent for "ductless mini split installation" everywhere.

  1. Audit the three signals. Proximity: single east-side location. Prominence: 60 reviews, moderate. Relevance: one "services" page listing eight services in two sentences each.
  2. Fix relevance. Build a full page for "ductless mini split installation Tucson" with real detail — sizing, brands, cost ranges, photos, FAQs. Set the GBP primary category correctly. Add neighborhood pages for the areas they actually serve west and north.
  3. Build prominence to overcome distance. Ask every mini-split customer for a review that mentions the service by name. Get listed in two or three local Tucson directories and a chamber page. Consistent name, address, phone everywhere.
  4. Accept the proximity ceiling. They will never beat a west-side competitor for "AC repair near me" from a west-side kitchen. That's fine. The specific, higher-value searches are where relevance and prominence win, and those searchers travel further anyway.

The point of the sequence: don't fight proximity with proximity. Fight it with the two levers you can actually move.

Reading the tradeoff with a simple diagnostic

You don't need software for the first pass. Run the same search from three points — your front door, two miles out, and five miles out — using an incognito window or a map-location check. Note where you drop off. Then do the same for one generic term and one specific term.

What this means for content specifically

Content is the relevance lever, and it quietly supports prominence too. Every page that answers a real question for a real service earns the chance to rank for that question's variations, picks up links when it's genuinely useful, and feeds internal links that strengthen your money pages. A page about "how long does a mini-split last in Arizona heat" won't book a job by itself, but it signals topical depth and gives you something to link from and earn links to.

That's the slow compounding part of local visibility most owners underestimate. Proximity is static. Prominence and relevance both grow with consistent, specific publishing — which is the work a program like ClearPath Content exists to run when you don't have time to map the question-space and write it yourself.

The practical takeaway

Stop treating your ranking as a single number. It's three levers recalculated for every searcher. Proximity you mostly inherit. Relevance you can fix in weeks with specific pages and the right profile categories. Prominence you build over months with reviews, citations, and links. When you lose a search, diagnose which lever is short before you spend a dollar — because the most common mistake is pouring effort into the one signal you can't change while ignoring the two you can.
