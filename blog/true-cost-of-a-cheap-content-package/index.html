What a $50 Blog Article Really Costs You

November 17, 2026 · 7 min read

content pricingbuying guidescontent marketingcheap content risksSEO content

A content vendor quotes you $50 an article. Another quotes $400. Same word count, same topic, same promise to "optimize for SEO." On a spreadsheet the first one wins by a mile. Twelve articles a month at $50 is $600. Twelve at $400 is $4,800. If you are running a dental practice or an HVAC company watching every line item, that gap is hard to ignore.

The problem is that the sticker price is the only cost that shows up before you buy. Everything else arrives later, in forms that do not look like invoices. This piece walks through where those costs actually land, with numbers you can run against your own quotes.

Where the money actually goes with cheap content

When someone sells a finished, SEO-ready article for $50, the writer is usually being paid somewhere between $8 and $25 for it. The rest covers the platform's margin. At that pay rate, a writer has to produce several articles a day to make a living. That math forces three things: speed over research, template over judgment, and volume over fit.

None of those are moral failings. They are the economics of the price point. You cannot pay a person $15 to spend four hours understanding your market, reading the top-ranking pages, and writing something a buyer would actually trust. So the vendor doesn't. What you get back reads fine on a first skim and falls apart the moment you ask it to do a job.

Here is the part that catches people. The $50 article is not free to fix. Your time, or your marketing lead's time, becomes the hidden second invoice.

The costs that don't show up on the quote

Break a cheap content package into the costs you actually absorb after delivery:

  • Editing and fact-checking time. Generic articles get details about your industry wrong. A piece on "when to replace an AC unit" that cites the wrong refrigerant rules, or a legal explainer that describes a statute that doesn't apply in your state, has to be caught and corrected by someone who knows the subject. That someone is usually you.
  • Rewrites for voice. Content that sounds like every other vendor's content doesn't sound like you. If your sales process depends on sounding like a specific kind of expert, a flat article undercuts it. Fixing tone takes nearly as long as writing fresh.
  • Opportunity cost of ranking nothing. This is the big one and the least visible. An article that targets a keyword 40 other pages already own, with no angle and no depth, will sit at position 60 forever. You paid for it, published it, and it brings zero traffic. The money is gone and so is the slot on your calendar.
  • Cleanup later. Thin content published at scale can drag on how search engines and AI answer tools read your whole site. Removing or rewriting 50 dead articles a year from now is a project nobody budgeted for.

A worked example

Suppose a law firm buys 10 articles a month at $50 each. The invoice is $500.

ItemTime or cost
Package price$500
Partner reviews 10 articles for legal accuracy, 30 min each5 hours
Marketing lead rewrites 4 for voice, 45 min each3 hours
2 articles pulled because they cite the wrong jurisdiction$100 wasted
8 published articles, 1 ranks anywhere useful after 6 months7 dead pages

If the partner's time is worth $300 an hour and the marketing lead's is worth $60, the review and rewrite labor alone is about $1,680. The real cost of that $500 package is closer to $2,180, and it produced one article that works. The firm would have been better off paying more for two articles that were right the first time.

Is cheap content ever the right call?

Cheap content is the right call when the stakes are low and the topic is simple enough that accuracy and voice barely matter. If you need internal product descriptions, basic category pages, or filler copy nobody will scrutinize, paying a premium is a waste. Volume at a low price is fine when a mistake costs you nothing.

The trouble is that most business owners buy cheap content for exactly the jobs where it fails: ranking against real competition, answering the questions buyers ask before they call, and representing expertise in a regulated or technical field. For those jobs the bargain price guarantees a bad outcome, because the thing you actually need — judgment about your market — is the thing that got cut to hit the price.

A simple test: if a wrong fact or a flat tone in this article could cost you a client, do not buy it cheap.

How do I tell a good content deal from a false bargain?

You tell a good deal from a false bargain by looking at what the vendor does before they write, not just what the finished article costs. Price per article tells you almost nothing. The process behind it tells you everything. Run any quote through this checklist:

  1. Ask who researches the topic. If the answer is "the writer, as they go," that is a volume shop. Good content starts with someone reading the current top-ranking pages and finding the gap, before a word is written.
  2. Ask how they handle your industry's specifics. Can they name the regulations, the seasonal patterns, the objections your buyers raise? If they can't ask good questions about your business, they can't write for it.
  3. Ask to see a sample in a field you know cold. Not their best polished sample — one in your industry. You will spot the fakery in 30 seconds because you know the subject.
  4. Ask what happens when an article doesn't rank. A serious vendor tracks performance and revises. A cheap one delivers and disappears.
  5. Ask how articles connect to each other. One-off articles with no internal linking or topic structure underperform a planned set, even if each piece is decent.

If a vendor passes those five, a higher per-article price is usually cheaper in total, because it removes the hidden labor and the dead pages from your side of the ledger.

The real number to compare

Stop comparing price per article. Compare cost per article that actually works. If a $50 package produces one useful piece out of ten, your real cost is $500 per working article, before your own labor. If a more expensive program produces six useful pieces out of eight, the math can flip entirely, even at four or five times the headline price.

This is why a planned monthly program — one that maps the questions your market actually searches, writes in your voice, and links the pieces into a structure — tends to beat a pile of cheap one-offs. ClearPath Content is built around that model for exactly this reason: the research and the structure are where the ranking comes from, and those are the first things a bargain package drops.

The takeaway: before you sign anything, take your lowest quote and add two columns — hours your team will spend fixing each article, and how many of the ten you honestly expect to rank. Do the same for the expensive quote. The cheaper package almost never wins once both columns are filled in. Price the outcome, not the invoice.
