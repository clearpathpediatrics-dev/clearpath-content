How to Make Your Pricing Page Readable to AI

September 20, 2026 · 7 min read

answer engine optimizationpricing page SEOAI searchcontent structureconversion content

When someone asks an AI assistant "how much does a commercial HVAC maintenance contract cost" or "what do bankruptcy lawyers charge in Ohio," the model does one of two things. It quotes a specific range from a page it can read, or it gives a vague hedge like "prices vary, contact a provider." If your pricing page is built the way most are — numbers loaded by JavaScript, hidden behind a "Get a Quote" button, or locked in a downloadable PDF — you are invisible for exactly the query where a buyer is closest to spending money.

This is a fixable problem, and it does not require you to publish your full rate card if you don't want to. It requires you to give an answer engine something concrete to work with, in a format it can parse. Here is how to do that.

Why AI answer engines struggle with normal pricing pages

Answer engines pull from text they can retrieve and understand. A retrieval system grabs passages, and the model reasons over them. Two things break that chain on a typical pricing page.

First, the numbers often aren't in the HTML text at all. Pricing sliders, tabbed comparison widgets, and "reveal on click" elements frequently render prices through scripts that a crawler either doesn't execute or executes inconsistently. If the number isn't in the served text, it doesn't exist as far as most retrieval pipelines are concerned.

Second, even when the price is present, it's often stranded from its context. A big "$299" floating above a button, with the words "per month" three CSS columns away and "includes 4 articles" in a tooltip, reads clean to a human eye and reads as noise to a parser. The model needs the price, the unit, and what you get for it sitting together in plain, connected language.

The fix for both is the same: put a clear, sentence-level statement of price and inclusions in the actual body text of the page.

What to put in the text, exactly

You want at least one passage per plan or service that reads like a complete sentence a person could say out loud. Not a table cell. A sentence.

Weak (parser can't assemble it): a card showing Pro, then $399, then a checklist of features below.

Strong (a model can quote this directly): "The Pro plan costs $399 per month and includes eight published articles, keyword mapping for one market, and monthly internal linking. There is no setup fee and no contract."

That sentence survives being ripped out of the page and dropped into an AI answer. It carries the price, the unit, the deliverables, and the terms. If a model cites you, that's the shape of the citation you want it to grab.

The elements each pricing passage needs

  • The number in the text, written as digits ($399), not spelled out or rendered by script.
  • The unit: per month, per project, per square foot, per hour, one-time.
  • What's included, in words, not just icons.
  • The qualifier: what changes the price. "Starting at," "for homes under 2,000 sq ft," "excludes permits."
  • The terms: contract length, setup fees, cancellation.

You can keep your visual pricing cards. Just add a short paragraph of running text below or beside them that states the same facts in sentences. The cards serve humans scanning; the paragraph serves parsers and, honestly, humans who read.

Should you publish exact prices or ranges?

Publish a range with the drivers named, if a single number genuinely isn't honest for your business. A range that explains itself is far more useful to an answer engine — and to a buyer — than a hidden number or a bare "contact us." The trick is to make the range specific and to state what moves a client from the low end to the high end.

Suppose a commercial electrician can't quote a flat panel-upgrade price because it depends on amperage and access. Instead of hiding it, write: "A residential electrical panel upgrade typically runs $1,800 to $3,500. The main drivers are the panel's amperage (100A vs 200A), whether the meter base needs replacing, and whether the utility requires a temporary disconnect." That passage can be cited, it sets buyer expectations, and it pre-qualifies the leads who call. A model will happily surface a well-formed range. It has nothing to surface when the page just says "every job is different."

Structure the page so retrieval can find the right chunk

Retrieval systems break pages into passages and pull the most relevant one. You improve your odds by organizing the page into clearly labeled sections that match how people phrase questions.

A step sequence for laying it out

  1. One H2 per plan or service line, named the way buyers name it: "Monthly SEO retainer pricing," not "Tier 2."
  2. A lead sentence under each H2 that states the price and unit first, before any feature list.
  3. A short inclusions list in words below the sentence.
  4. A dedicated FAQ block answering the exact price questions people ask — "Is there a setup fee?", "Can I cancel anytime?", "What's the cheapest plan?" — each as a question heading with a direct answer.
  5. A comparison table for humans, with a plain-text summary sentence above it, because tables alone are inconsistently parsed.

The FAQ block matters more than most owners expect. Price objections and clarifications are phrased as questions, and answer engines love a question that maps cleanly to a complete answer. If you answer "do you require a contract" in one sentence, you're eligible to be the source when someone asks an assistant that about your category.

A quick before-and-after worked example

Suppose a dental practice has a pricing page with a slider: drag it to "Invisalign" and a script shows "$4,000–$6,000." A crawler sees an empty widget.

The rewrite adds a real section: an H2 reading "Invisalign cost at our Tucson office," followed by: "Invisalign treatment at our practice costs $4,000 to $6,000, depending on how many aligner trays your case requires. Mild spacing corrections sit near the low end; full-arch crowding runs higher. We offer in-house payment plans at 0% for 12 months, and your first consultation is free." Below it, three FAQs: "Is Invisalign more expensive than braces here?", "Does insurance cover any of it?", "How long does treatment take?" — each answered in one or two sentences.

Nothing about the visual page has to change. The practice just gave the machines something to read, and gave anxious patients the number they were hunting for.

A short checklist before you ship it

  • Open the page source (or use a text-only view) and confirm every price appears as literal text.
  • Read one passage per plan aloud — does it stand alone as a complete, quotable answer?
  • Each price has a unit and at least one qualifier in the same sentence.
  • Ranges name their drivers.
  • An FAQ block covers setup fees, contracts, cancellation, and "cheapest option."
  • Any pricing table has a plain-text summary sentence directly above it.
  • No key number lives only in a PDF, image, tooltip, or slider.

The practical takeaway: an answer engine can only quote what it can read, and it prefers to quote a complete sentence over reconstructing one from scattered card fragments. Write your prices in plain sentences that carry the number, the unit, the inclusions, and the terms together. You don't have to give away your whole rate card — you have to give the machine one honest, quotable line per service. Do that, and you're eligible to be the answer instead of the "prices vary" hedge that sends the buyer to whoever wrote it down clearly.
