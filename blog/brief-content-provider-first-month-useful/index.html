How to Brief a Content Provider So Month One Pays Off

November 5, 2026 · 7 min read

content briefingcontent marketinghiring a content agencycontent strategyonboarding a writer

Most of the disappointment people feel in month one with a content provider traces back to one thing: the brief was thin. The provider filled the gaps with assumptions, and the assumptions were generic because they didn't know your business yet. You get four articles that read like they could belong to any company in your industry, and now you're editing instead of publishing.

The fix isn't writing a 20-page strategy document. It's handing over a specific set of inputs that only you have, in a form the provider can actually use. This piece walks through exactly what to give them, what order to do it in, and how to spot in week two whether the first month is going to be useful or not.

Why the first month usually underwhelms

A content provider starts with public information: your website, your competitors' pages, keyword tools, whatever shows up in search. That gets them to a competent-but-generic baseline. The difference between generic and useful lives in your head and in your sales records — the exact phrasing customers use, the objections you hear weekly, the jobs you want more of, the ones you want fewer of.

If you don't transfer that, the provider can't guess it. They'll write a solid article on "how much does X cost" that never mentions the three pricing questions your phone staff answer every single day. It's not wrong. It's just not yours. The brief is where you close that gap, and month one is when it matters most because everything after builds on what gets established now.

The inputs that actually change the output

Give these five things up front. They do more than any amount of back-and-forth on tone.

1. Your top 10 real customer questions

Not keywords — actual questions, in the words customers use. Ask whoever answers your phone to jot down what people ask for one week. You'll get phrasing like "do I need to be home for the whole thing" or "will my insurance cover this." Those are worth more than any keyword list because they're the gap between what you sell and what people are unsure about.

2. The jobs you want more of (and less of)

If you're an HVAC company that makes money on installs and loses money on cheap repair calls, say so. A provider who doesn't know this will happily rank you for "$49 furnace tune-up" and bring you exactly the wrong leads. Tell them what a good customer looks like and what a good job pays.

3. Three competitors you respect and one you don't

Naming competitors tells the provider who to study and who to differentiate from. The one you don't respect is useful too — it usually surfaces a positioning point ("we actually show up when we say we will") that becomes a theme worth writing around.

4. Proof you can point to

Certifications, years in business, warranty terms, service area, number of jobs completed, named equipment brands you carry. These are the concrete details that make a page specific instead of fluffy. Hand over a plain list. The writer will fold them in where they fit.

5. A voice sample you'd actually approve

Send two or three things you've written or said that sound like you — an email to a customer, a page you like, even a transcript of you explaining your service to someone. One real sample beats a paragraph of adjectives like "professional but friendly," which every client says and which means nothing to a writer.

What should I send a content provider before they start writing?

Send your top customer questions, your ideal-job profile, named competitors, a proof list, and a genuine voice sample — in that order of priority. The customer questions shape what gets written, the ideal-job profile shapes who it's aimed at, the competitors set the bar, the proof makes it specific, and the voice sample keeps it sounding like you. If you only have time for one thing, make it the customer questions written in the exact language people use. Everything else can be refined in month two; the question list is what prevents the generic first draft that wastes the whole first month.

A week-by-week look at month one

Here's how a well-briefed first month tends to run, so you know what to watch for.

  1. Week 1 — brief and plan. You hand over the five inputs. The provider comes back with a proposed list of topics and the questions each will answer. Read this list. If the topics don't map to things you actually want to rank for or sell, say so now. Fixing a plan takes five minutes; fixing four finished articles takes a month.
  2. Week 2 — first draft. You get one or two pieces, not all four. This is deliberate. Review the first draft hard, because your feedback here sets the pattern for everything else. Mark what sounds off, what's missing, what a customer would actually ask next.
  3. Week 3 — correction and cadence. The remaining pieces come back reflecting your week-2 notes. If they don't — if you're flagging the same problem twice — that's your early signal to have a direct conversation.
  4. Week 4 — published and interlinked. Articles are live, linked to each other and to the pages that make you money. You should be able to read each one and think, "a customer would find this useful," not just "this reads fine."

The important mechanic here is getting a sample early. A provider who sends all four articles at once at the end of the month has given you no chance to steer. Ask for the first piece by week two specifically so you can correct course before the pattern is set.

How do I know if the first month's content is any good?

Read one article as if you were a customer and ask whether it answers the next question you'd have — not whether it's grammatically clean or hits a word count. Good first-month content names specific things only your business would know: your actual service area, the brands you carry, the real objection you hear, the price range you actually charge within. Generic content hedges everywhere and could be lifted onto a competitor's site with a find-and-replace. A second test: does it link to the page you want people to call from, with language that gives them a reason to click? If the article is a dead end that ranks but never points anywhere, it's doing half the job. Clean writing is table stakes; specificity and a clear next step are what tell you the brief landed.

A short worked example

Suppose a dental practice in Columbus signs up and sends a thin brief — just "we do implants and cosmetic work, professional tone." The provider writes a competent "dental implants cost" article built from keyword data. It's fine and it's forgettable.

Now suppose the same practice sends the five inputs. The question list reveals patients keep asking "can I get this done in one visit" and "what if I've been told I don't have enough bone." The ideal-job note says they want full-arch cases, not single crowns. The proof list includes an in-house CT scanner and a specific implant brand. The voice sample is a reassuring consultation email.

The article that comes back now opens by addressing the bone-grafting worry, explains the one-visit question honestly, mentions the in-house scanner as the reason they can assess candidacy same-day, and points toward booking a consultation. Same topic. Completely different result — because the inputs were specific. Providers like ClearPath Content can only reflect back what you put in; the brief is the lever that decides what comes out.

The takeaway

Treat the brief as the highest-leverage hour of the whole engagement. Spend it collecting real customer questions, defining the jobs you want, and handing over proof and a voice sample — then insist on seeing a sample draft by week two so you can correct before the pattern sets. Do that, and month one stops being a test you hope passes and becomes the month your content actually started sounding like your business.
