How to Find the Questions Your Buyers Type

September 9, 2026 · 7 min read

keyword researchbuyer intentquestion researchcontent strategyorganic visibility

Most keyword research produces a spreadsheet of nouns. "HVAC repair." "Estate planning." "Payroll software." Those are categories, not questions. The person searching "HVAC repair" might be a homeowner whose furnace died, a landlord comparing service contracts, or a student writing a paper. You can't write one useful article for all three.

The questions your buyers actually type are more specific and more revealing: "why is my furnace blowing cold air," "how much does it cost to replace a capacitor," "do I need a permit to replace a water heater in Ohio." Those phrases tell you the buyer's situation, their stage, and often their budget. Finding them is the difference between content that ranks for vanity terms and content that pulls in people ready to call you.

Here is how to find the real phrasing, mostly from sources you already own.

Start with the questions you're already answering

Before you touch a keyword tool, mine the conversations your business is already having. These are the exact words your buyers use, unfiltered by any tool's guesswork.

Sales and intake calls

Ask whoever answers the phone to keep a running note for two weeks: every question a prospect asks before they buy. Not a paraphrase — the actual wording. "Can you come out same day?" "Do you work with my insurance?" "What's the difference between a tune-up and a full service?" These are questions with commercial intent, because the person asking is already talking to you.

Support tickets and emails

Search your inbox and help desk for question marks. Sort by frequency. If eleven people asked some version of "how long does the process take," that is a page. The phrasing repeats because the confusion is real.

Your own FAQ and the ones you wish you had

Look at the last twenty deals that stalled. What did the prospect not understand? What objection came up? Each one is a question someone is typing into a search bar right now, hoping a competitor answers it before you do.

Use free tools that surface real query phrasing

Once you've drained your internal sources, expand with tools that show how strangers phrase the same problems. You don't need a paid platform to start.

  • Google autocomplete. Type your core term plus "how," "why," "can," "does," "is," and "cost." The suggestions are drawn from real query volume. Type "water heater why" and Google fills in the rest.
  • People Also Ask. The expandable question boxes on a results page are a live map of related questions. Click one and it spawns more. Screenshot or copy every relevant one.
  • Related searches. Scroll to the bottom of any results page. These are adjacent phrasings, often longer and more specific than your seed term.
  • Reddit and niche forums. Search "[your topic] site:reddit.com" and read how people describe their problem in their own words. Forum titles are questions, phrased naturally, with no marketing polish.
  • Free keyword tools. AnswerThePublic, Keyword Surfer, and Google's own Keyword Planner (free with an ads account) all group queries by question modifier.

Copy everything into one document. Don't filter yet. You're collecting raw language first, judging it second.

How do I know which questions are worth writing about?

Score each question on three things: intent, volume, and your ability to answer it better than what's ranking now. A question worth writing about is one where the searcher is close to needing what you sell, enough people ask it to matter, and the current top results are thin, generic, or out of date.

Run each candidate question through this quick filter:

  1. Intent. Is the person asking this likely to become a customer? "How much does a new roof cost" signals a buyer. "How are roof shingles made" signals a browser. Favor the buyer.
  2. Volume, roughly. You don't need exact numbers. If autocomplete suggests it, People Also Ask shows it, and it came up on real calls, that's enough signal. Consistent low-volume buyer questions beat high-volume tire-kicker terms.
  3. Competition and quality. Search the question. If the top results are a national directory and two out-of-date blog posts, a specific, current answer from a real local business can compete. If the top results are already excellent, pick a narrower angle.

Kill anything that fails intent, no matter how much volume it has. Traffic that never converts costs you money to produce and maintain.

A worked example: a plumbing company in Tucson

Suppose a plumbing company in Tucson wants a month of content. Here's the sequence.

Step 1 — Mine internal sources. The office manager logs two weeks of calls. Recurring questions: "Do you charge for estimates?" "How fast can you fix a burst pipe?" "Can you install a tankless water heater in an older house?"

Step 2 — Expand with autocomplete. Typing "tankless water heater" plus modifiers returns: "tankless water heater worth it," "tankless water heater cost to install," "tankless water heater problems hard water." That last one matters — Tucson has hard water, and it connects a general question to a local reality.

Step 3 — Check People Also Ask. Searching "tankless water heater cost" surfaces "how long do tankless water heaters last" and "do tankless heaters need special venting." Both go on the list.

Step 4 — Score and select. The team picks four questions with clear buyer intent and beatable competition:

QuestionIntentCompetition
How much does tankless installation cost in Tucson?HighWeak locally
Do tankless water heaters work with hard water?Medium-highGeneric only
How fast can a plumber fix a burst pipe?High (urgent)Thin
Do plumbers charge for estimates?MediumMixed

Step 5 — Write the answer, not the keyword. Each article opens by answering the question in the first two sentences, then goes deeper. The cost article gives a real range and explains what moves the price. It doesn't pad with the history of hot water.

Four articles, all drawn from questions the business was already fielding by phone. No guessing about what a stranger might want.

Should I write for one big keyword or many specific questions?

Write for many specific questions, because that's how people actually search and how AI answer engines pull responses. A single broad keyword like "plumbing services" is nearly impossible to rank for and vague when you do. A cluster of specific question-pages each answers a real need, ranks faster, and gives search and AI engines clean, quotable answers to surface. Over time those specific pages link to each other and to a broader overview page, and the overview borrows authority from all of them.

The old model chased one high-volume term. The current reality rewards depth across a question-space. Ten precise answers beat one broad essay almost every time.

Turn the list into a publishing plan

Once you have thirty or forty scored questions, group them by theme — cost, process, comparison, urgency, local regulations. Each theme becomes a small cluster. Order them by intent, publishing the highest-intent buyer questions first so the earliest work has the best chance of returning something.

Keep the raw phrasing in your titles and opening lines where it reads naturally. If people type "how much does it cost to replace a water heater," a headline using those exact words matches better than "Water Heater Replacement Pricing Guide."

The takeaway: your buyers have already told you what they're searching for. It's in your call logs, your inbox, and your stalled deals. Pull that language first, confirm it with free tools, score it for intent, and write the answer plainly. You'll spend less time guessing and more time answering questions people are actually typing.
