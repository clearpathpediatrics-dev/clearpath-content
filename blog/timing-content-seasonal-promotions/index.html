How to Time Content Around Seasonal Promotions

November 19, 2026 · 7 min read

seasonal content planningcontent operationspromotional SEOcontent calendareditorial planning

Most seasonal promotions fail on timing, not offer. The discount is fine. The landing page is fine. The problem is that the supporting content went live the same week as the sale, which means Google had no time to crawl, index, and rank it before demand peaked. By the time the article starts pulling traffic, the promotion is over.

Search content is slow. Promotions are fast. Coordinating the two means working backward from the date customers start searching, not the date your sale starts. Those are rarely the same day.

Why seasonal content needs a head start

A new article does not rank the day you publish it. It needs to be crawled, indexed, and then assessed against competing pages over a stretch of weeks. For a low-competition question on an established domain, that can be fast. For anything commercial, where other businesses are also trying to rank, it takes longer and the position keeps moving for a while after publication.

Now layer the demand curve on top. Searches for "AC tune-up" do not spike on the first hot day. They build through late spring as people anticipate summer. If your tune-up promotion content publishes in June, you have missed the climb. The traffic you wanted went to pages that were already ranking in April and May.

The fix is to separate two dates in your head: the search date (when people start looking) and the sale date (when your offer runs). Content targets the search date. The promotion rides on top of content that is already ranking.

Build the timeline backward from peak search

Start with when your audience actually searches, then count backward. Here is a working example for a hypothetical HVAC company running a spring tune-up promotion.

Step 1 — Find the search ramp. Spring maintenance searches in a warm-climate market tend to climb from March and peak in May–June. Treat early May as peak.

Step 2 — Give the content runway. Commercial articles need weeks of indexing and settling before they hold a useful position. Publish by early March at the latest, which gives roughly 8–10 weeks before peak.

Step 3 — Schedule the writing. If articles publish in early March, drafts need to be done in February, which means the topics and briefs are set in January.

Step 4 — Attach the promotion last. The actual sale might run for two weeks in May. By then the articles have been live for two months and are already catching traffic. You add a promotional call-to-action and an offer section to pages that are already performing.

The sequence looks like this:

WhenAction
JanuaryPick topics, write briefs, map internal links
FebruaryDraft and edit articles
Early MarchPublish all supporting content
March–AprilPages index, rank, accumulate traffic
Early MayAdd offer CTAs; launch promotion

The mistake businesses make is collapsing all of this into May. The article, the landing page, and the sale all go live together, and nothing has had time to rank.

The offer page

This is the transactional page with the discount, the terms, and the booking form. It does not need to rank for much on its own. It needs to convert people who are already sold. Keep it simple and keep the URL stable so you can reuse it season over season instead of building a new one each time.

The feeder articles

These are the informational pieces that answer the questions people ask before they are ready to buy. For the tune-up example: "how often should you service an air conditioner," "signs your AC needs maintenance before summer," "what's included in an AC tune-up." These rank for the searches happening during the ramp, and each one links to the offer page.

The feeder articles are what you publish in March. They do the slow work of earning position. When the promotion launches, you add a short offer block near the top of each one and let existing traffic flow to the offer page. You are not starting from zero in May — you are monetizing attention you already built.

When should I start creating content for a seasonal promotion?

Start creating content eight to twelve weeks before customers begin searching, not before your sale begins. Commercial articles need weeks to index and settle into a ranking position, and seasonal demand usually ramps up well ahead of the peak. Work backward: identify when search interest starts climbing in your market, subtract two to three months for writing and indexing, and set your publish date there. The promotion itself gets attached later, to pages that are already live and ranking. If you wait until the sale is scheduled to start writing, the content will not rank in time to matter for that season.

Should I make a new page for each seasonal sale or reuse one?

Reuse one stable offer page for recurring seasonal promotions rather than building a new URL each time. A page that has been live for several seasons has accumulated links, history, and whatever ranking signals it earned, all of which reset to zero when you create a fresh URL. Keep a permanent page for the promotion type — for example, /ac-tune-up-special — and update the offer, dates, and pricing inside it each season. Point your feeder articles at that same URL every year. The exception is a genuinely one-off event with no future equivalent, where a disposable page is fine. For anything that returns annually, a permanent page compounds; a new one starts over.

A reusable seasonal content checklist

Run this for each seasonal promotion you plan:

  • Identify the search ramp. When does interest start climbing in your market? That is your target, not the sale date.
  • Set the publish date. Eight to twelve weeks before the ramp for commercial topics.
  • List 3–5 feeder questions. Real questions buyers ask before this purchase.
  • Confirm the offer page exists and is stable. Reuse last year's URL if you have one.
  • Write and publish feeders early. Get them indexed before demand builds.
  • Interlink. Every feeder links to the offer page; the offer page links back to the most useful feeders.
  • Add the offer block at launch. Insert the CTA into ranking pages when the sale goes live.
  • Leave the feeders up after the sale. Swap the offer block for an evergreen CTA. They keep ranking for next year.

That last point matters. The feeder articles are not disposable. Strip out the dated offer when the promotion ends, leave a standard "book a service" link, and the page keeps working. Next season you reactivate the offer block on a page that already ranks. Each year the groundwork gets lighter.

The common failure, stated plainly

Businesses treat promotional content as an announcement — something you publish to tell people the sale is happening. Search does not work on announcement speed. By the time an announcement-style page ranks, the thing it announced is over. Content coordinated with seasonal promotions works on anticipation speed: you publish before the question peaks, earn position during the ramp, and attach the offer to attention you have already captured.

If planning this backward across several promotions a year gets unwieldy, that mapping and cadence is the kind of thing a content program like ClearPath Content handles — setting brief dates and publish dates against each season's search ramp so nothing goes live too late.

The takeaway: Pick your next seasonal promotion and write down two dates — when people start searching and when your sale runs. Count back two to three months from the search date and put the publish deadline on your calendar now. That one habit fixes the most common reason seasonal content underperforms.
