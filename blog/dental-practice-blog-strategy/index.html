What Should a Dental Practice Blog About?

August 29, 2026 · 8 min read

dental blog strategydental practice marketingcontent marketing for dentistsdental SEOlocal search for dentists

Most dental practice blogs read like a first-year hygiene textbook. "The Importance of Flossing." "5 Foods That Stain Your Teeth." These posts feel productive to write, and they do almost nothing, because the people searching for them are students and the mildly curious, not patients looking for a dentist. A blog that brings in patients targets the questions people ask when they are deciding to book, comparing options, or worried about something specific happening in their mouth right now.

This piece walks through how to choose those topics, how to structure the posts so they answer real decisions, and a starter plan you can hand to whoever writes for you.

Start with the three question-types that precede a booking

Every patient who books online passed through some version of a search first. There are three buckets worth writing for, and they map to how close someone is to picking up the phone.

Procedure-decision questions

These are the highest-value posts. Someone is weighing a specific treatment and wants to understand cost, pain, recovery, and alternatives before they commit. Examples: "how much does a dental implant cost," "is Invisalign worth it for adults," "root canal vs extraction." People searching these are often within weeks of booking. They are also searching with intent to spend real money.

Symptom and worry questions

Someone has a problem and does not yet know what it means. "Why does my tooth hurt when I bite down," "gums bleeding when I brush," "white spot on my gum." These bring in people who need care now. The trick is to answer the question honestly and then explain when it warrants a visit, without turning every post into a fear pitch.

Local and logistics questions

"Emergency dentist open Saturday," "dentist that takes [insurance] near me," "how to switch dentists." These are lower in volume but convert well because the searcher has already decided they need a dentist and is sorting out the practical details.

If you only ever write from these three buckets, you will outperform a practice publishing generic oral-hygiene tips every week.

What should a dental practice blog about?

A dental practice blog should focus on the specific procedures you offer, the symptoms your ideal patients search for, and the local logistics that come up when someone chooses a dentist. Prioritize procedure-decision content for treatments with higher margins and longer consideration periods, like implants, cosmetic work, and orthodontics, because those searchers are actively comparing providers and researching cost. Then cover the common symptoms that lead to appointments, and round it out with posts answering practical questions about insurance, emergencies, hours, and new-patient logistics. Avoid broad educational topics that attract readers who will never become patients.

Write each post around a decision, not a keyword

A keyword tells you what to name the file. A decision tells you what to write. Take "how much does a dental implant cost." A weak post lists a national price range and stops. A strong post answers the actual decision the reader is making, which is "should I do this, and what will it really involve for me?"

That means covering:

  • The realistic cost range and the factors that move it (bone grafting, number of teeth, materials)
  • What the cost includes and what shows up as a surprise line item later
  • The alternatives and why someone would choose them (bridge, denture, doing nothing)
  • What the process looks like from consultation to final crown, with rough timeframes
  • Financing or insurance realities, stated plainly

You do not have to publish your exact prices, and many practices won't. But you can give ranges and explain the variables so the reader trusts you before they call. A post that pretends everything is simple reads like a sales page. A post that admits the tradeoffs reads like a dentist you'd want to see.

How often should a dental office publish blog posts?

A dental office should aim for two to four well-researched posts per month rather than a high volume of thin ones. Consistency matters more than frequency, and a small practice that publishes two genuinely useful 900-to-1,400-word posts every month will build authority faster than one that dumps ten short posts and then goes quiet for a quarter. Search engines and patients both reward depth and steadiness. If you can only sustain two posts a month, plan for two. The most common failure is not publishing too little, it is starting at eight posts a month, burning out by month two, and abandoning the blog entirely.

A 12-post starter plan

Suppose a general and cosmetic practice in a mid-sized suburb wants a quarter of content. Here is a plan that mixes the three buckets and front-loads the high-value procedure posts. At two posts a month, this is six months of work; at four a month, it's three.

1How much do dental implants cost in [city] and what affects the priceProcedure
2Invisalign vs braces for adults: cost, time, and resultsProcedure
3Why does my tooth hurt when I bite down?Symptom
4Root canal or extraction: how to decideProcedure
5What to do when a crown falls outSymptom
6Teeth whitening options: what actually works and what's a wasteProcedure
7Finding an emergency dentist in [city] on nights and weekendsLocal
8Bleeding gums: when it's normal and when it's gum diseaseSymptom
9What a new-patient visit at our office actually involvesLocal
10Veneers vs bonding for a chipped front toothProcedure
11How to switch dentists without losing your recordsLocal
12Does dental insurance cover implants? How to check your planLogistics

Notice the ratio: six procedure posts, three symptom posts, three local/logistics posts. That weighting reflects where the revenue is. Adjust it to your practice. A pediatric office would swap in parent-anxiety topics; an office that does a lot of emergency work would add more same-day posts.

Interlink so the blog behaves like a system

Individual posts help. Connected posts compound. When your implant-cost post links to your "root canal or extraction" post and your insurance post, a reader deciding to replace a tooth can move through three related pages without leaving your site, and search engines read the cluster as a sign you cover the subject thoroughly.

A simple rule: every new post should link to at least two existing posts, and you should go back and add a link to the new post from one older, related post. That last step is the one everyone skips, and it's why most practice blogs are a pile of orphaned pages instead of a network.

Keep it in your voice and factually clean

Dental content carries a real accuracy burden. If you outsource writing, someone clinical on your side needs to review claims about procedures, recovery times, and risks before anything publishes. A confident-sounding post with a wrong recovery timeline erodes trust faster than no post at all. Build a fifteen-minute review step into every piece where a dentist or hygienist reads it once for accuracy.

The other honesty test is tone. Patients can smell a post written to scare them into booking. Answer the question they asked, tell them when something genuinely needs a dentist and when it can wait, and let the competence do the selling.

The takeaway

Stop writing for the curious and start writing for the deciding. Pick your highest-value procedures, answer the real questions people ask before they book, keep a sustainable cadence of two to four solid posts a month, link them into clusters, and have someone clinical check the facts. Do that for a year and you'll have a blog that quietly brings patients in the door instead of one that just proves you have a blog.
