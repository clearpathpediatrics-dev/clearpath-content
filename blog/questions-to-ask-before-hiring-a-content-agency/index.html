12 Questions to Ask Before Hiring a Content Agency

September 12, 2026 · 7 min read

hiring a content agencycontent agency questionscontent marketing vettingbuying guidesSEO agency evaluation

Most content agencies pass the first meeting. They have a polished deck, a few logos, and a confident answer to "what's your process." The problem shows up in month four, when you've paid for a dozen articles that read fine and rank for nothing. By then you've lost a quarter and part of a budget you can't get back.

The way to avoid that is to ask better questions before you sign. Not trick questions — questions that force the agency to show you the mechanics behind the pitch. Below are the ones worth asking, grouped by what they're actually testing, along with the answers that should make you keep looking.

Questions about how they choose topics

This is the single most predictive area. An agency that can't explain its topic selection is going to write whatever it can produce quickly, and volume without intent is the most common way content money disappears.

1. How do you decide what to write about?

You want a specific method, not "we research your industry." Good answers reference keyword and question research, mapping the questions your buyers actually type, checking what already ranks, and prioritizing topics by commercial intent. If the answer is "we'll brainstorm topics with you each month," that means the work is coming out of your head, and you're paying them to type.

2. How do you tell the difference between a topic that converts and one that just gets traffic?

Listen for the distinction between informational and commercial intent. A plumbing company doesn't need an article titled "history of indoor plumbing" — it needs "how much does it cost to replace a water heater in [city]." If the agency talks only about traffic numbers and never about who's reading and whether they buy, that's a flag.

Questions about who actually writes

3. Who writes the articles, and where are they based?

You're allowed to ask this directly. Some agencies use in-house writers, some use vetted freelancers, some use offshore mills, some run everything through AI with light editing. None of these is automatically disqualifying — a strong AI-assisted process with real editorial review can be fine — but you need to know so you can judge the price and the quality against it. An agency dodging this question is hiding something about margin or method.

4. Can I see three samples in a voice like mine, plus the brief that produced one of them?

Samples alone are easy to fake with cherry-picked work. The brief is the tell. A real brief shows the target question, the search intent, the intended reader, the internal links, and the angle. If they can produce the brief behind a good article, they have a system. If they can only show you the finished piece, you're seeing the exception, not the process.

5. How do you learn and keep my brand voice?

Look for a documented voice guide, not "we'll get a feel for it." A firm that takes voice seriously will interview you or your team, pull phrasing from your existing material, and produce a reference document writers work from. Without that, every writer guesses, and your blog reads like four different people wrote it, because they did.

Questions about what you actually get

6. What exactly is included each month, and what counts as one deliverable?

Get this in writing. "Four articles a month" can mean 600-word posts or 1,600-word researched pieces. Ask about word count ranges, revisions, images, internal linking, publishing, and whether meta descriptions and titles are included. Vague scope is where the relationship sours.

7. Do you publish the content or just hand it over?

Handoff-only means the work stops the moment the doc lands in your inbox, and if you have no marketing team, those docs pile up unpublished. If publishing is included, ask how — do they need access to your CMS, do they format for the web, do they add the internal links themselves. This is the difference between a program that runs and a folder of unused drafts.

8. How do the articles connect to each other?

Strong content isn't a list of unrelated posts. Ask whether they build clusters — a central page on a broad topic supported by focused articles that link back to it — and whether they interlink new pieces with old ones. A firm that treats each article as a standalone unit is leaving most of the value on the table, because interlinked content builds topical depth that isolated posts never will.

Questions about ownership and exit

9. Who owns the content if I cancel?

You should own everything you paid for, full stop. Get it in the contract. Some agencies retain rights or host content on their own subdomains, which means canceling deletes your library. Ask directly: "If I leave, do the articles stay on my site and belong to me?" The answer should be an immediate yes.

10. What's the minimum commitment, and what happens in the first 90 days?

Organic content takes months to compound, so some commitment is reasonable — but a 12-month lock with no early checkpoint favors the agency, not you. Ask what the first 90 days look like and what you should expect to see by then. A good answer talks about leading indicators (pages indexed, early impressions, keywords starting to register) rather than promising rankings, because nobody honest promises rankings.

Questions about measurement

11. How will we know if this is working, and when?

The honest answer is that meaningful organic movement usually takes several months, and the early signals are impressions, indexing, and keyword coverage before they become clicks and leads. Be wary of anyone who promises a specific traffic number or a first-page ranking by a specific date. Also be wary of anyone who only reports vanity metrics — total pageviews with no connection to intent or conversions.

12. Will you report on leads or conversions, not just traffic?

The best agencies tie their reporting to business outcomes: form fills, calls, demo requests, or at least assisted conversions. If they can only show you traffic graphs going up, you have no way to know whether any of it matters. Ask what they'll report and how often.

A quick scoring checklist

Run each candidate through this before your second call. Give one point per solid answer:

  • Explains topic selection with a real research method
  • Distinguishes commercial from informational intent
  • Names who writes and where
  • Can show a brief, not just a sample
  • Uses a documented voice guide
  • Defines the monthly deliverable precisely
  • Includes publishing, or is clear that it doesn't
  • Builds and interlinks clusters
  • Confirms you own the content on exit
  • Offers a 90-day checkpoint
  • Frames results around leading indicators, no ranking promises
  • Reports on conversions, not just traffic

Nine or more is a serious candidate. Six to eight, keep them on the list but probe the gaps. Below six, you're likely buying word count.

The practical takeaway

Send your three finalists the same five questions by email before you book a call: how they pick topics, who writes, what one deliverable includes, who owns the content on exit, and how they measure results. Compare the written answers side by side. The agency that answers plainly and specifically, without hedging or upselling, is almost always the one worth your time — and the exercise costs you nothing but a short email.
